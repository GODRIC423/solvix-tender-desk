Essay · Neoplasticism, mechanised

A painting that
redraws itself

In 1917 a handful of Dutch painters, architects and one very opinionated typographer decided that art had been carrying too much luggage. Piet Mondrian, Theo van Doesburg and Bart van der Leck stripped painting down to what they thought could not be removed: the horizontal, the vertical, the three primaries, and the two non-colours that hold them apart. They called it Nieuwe Beelding, the New Plastic, and their magazine was called De Stijl. The Style. Definite article.

What the rules were for

It is tempting to read Mondrian's grids as decoration, because they became decoration: dresses, coffee mugs, an entire Dutch railway station. But he meant them as a kind of moral argument. A diagonal, he thought, was a compromise; a curve was a sentimental detour; a mixed colour was a colour that had lost its nerve. Reduce everything to the perpendicular and the primary and what remains is pure relationship, the balance of unequal things. He called that dynamic equilibrium, and it took him weeks of repainting a single black bar a centimetre to the left to find it.

Why a machine

Mondrian would have hated this page. He broke with van Doesburg in 1925 over a single tilted line, and he never used a ruler-drawn grid; every bar was painted by hand and adjusted by eye. So why simulate him? Because the rules he wrote down turn out to be an unusually good specification. Most art movements leave behind a mood. Neoplasticism left behind constraints, and constraints are the one thing a computer is genuinely good at obeying.

What the machine cannot do is stop. Mondrian's compositions end at the exact moment the balance locks. This one just keeps proposing. That gap, between a proposal and a decision, is the whole difference between a generator and a painter, and it is worth watching for while you press the key.

Mondrian never painted on a white canvas: his whites are five or six different whites, each mixed for its neighbours. The machine cheats. It has one.

The eleven rules the generator obeys

  1. Rectilinear only. Every plane is a rectangle whose edges lie on the shared grid. There is no element on this page with a border-radius, including the favicon.
  2. Fixed track count, variable weight. The grid always has seven columns and six rows so that its templates can animate. Only the proportions change.
  3. Unequal tracks. Each track is drawn from a wide range of fractional units, so no two compositions share a rhythm and nothing is ever centred.
  4. Planes may span. A plane can cover up to five columns and four rows, but never the entire width. A painting needs more than one thing in it.
  5. Black is a bar, not a plane. The gaps between planes are the black lines. They have one weight, the way Mondrian's did after 1921.
  6. White dominates. Roughly three fifths to three quarters of the planes are white. Colour is punctuation, not prose.
  7. All three primaries appear. A composition without red, yellow and blue is regenerated before you see it.
  8. No colour touches itself. Two planes of the same primary never share an edge. The machine checks its neighbours before it commits.
  9. The largest plane is white. The biggest rectangle carries no colour, so weight comes from placement rather than area.
  10. One black plane, sometimes. At most a single small black plane may appear, as a full stop.
  11. Motion is transition. Nothing eases along a curve of position. Track sizes interpolate and colours cross-fade; the planes themselves snap into place.

“The straight line tells the truth.”

Theo van Doesburg, De Stijl, 1918 · paraphrased into a slogan by everyone since

Reading a result

Try this: generate until something makes you pause, then ask why. Usually it is one of three things. A tall red plane against a wide field of white feels loud because the grid is quiet. A blue plane pinned in a corner drags the eye off the canvas, which Mondrian did deliberately to suggest the painting continued past its edge. A yellow plane between two heavy black bars looks like light between shutters. None of this is in the code. The code only forbids; the meaning is what survives the forbidding.

That, in the end, was the De Stijl wager: that if you remove enough, what is left will have to mean something. A hundred years later, on a screen redrawing itself in under a second, it still mostly does.
