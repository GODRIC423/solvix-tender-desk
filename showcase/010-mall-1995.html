A poem for a room of two hundred bolted chairs, written from memory and the smell of teriyaki samples on toothpicks.

Under a skylight the color of weak tea, two hundred chairs bolted to their tables so no one could ever move closer to anyone.

Sbarro breathing its orange breath, ピザ the pretzel man salting a sky of dough, a lady in a paper hat holding out chicken like a question no one was rude enough to refuse.

We were fourteen and rich with four dollars. We split a large Coke five ways and called it lunch. Somebody's older brother had a car; we said this often, as if the car were a country we would someday visit.

Now the ficus trees are plastic and still alive, the fountain is a dry mint-colored bowl, and the speakers in the ceiling keep playing a saxophone for a crowd that grew up and drove home.

Nothing here makes a sound. The titles are fictional but the feeling is documented: in 1995 the Muzak corporation piped licensed "environmental" programming into roughly 250,000 locations, sequenced in fifteen-minute blocks that rose in tempo toward the top of every hour. When the mall died, the contract did not. Somewhere a speaker is still under it.

Dead malls are not haunted; that would require someone to have left. What they are is paused. The escalators are switched off mid-step. The directory still shows you where you are. The word SALE is still taped inside a window that has been dark for two decades, and the tape has gone the color of a nicotine ceiling.

This page is a small act of upkeep: a directory for a building that no longer needs one, a poem for a food court that no longer feeds, and a playlist for speakers that have nobody to play to. Hover over anything. It will flicker. That is how you know it is still on.
