On the Aerodynamics of Paper Planes: a Minimal Drag–Lift Model with Two Theorems and a Live Figure

Marguerite Okonkwo1·Leander Berthold1·Rosalind Sainte-Marie2

1Laboratory for Folded Flight, Department of Applied Idleness, University of the Corridor
2Institute of Low Reynolds Numbers, Mont-Saint-Aubin

3 September 2026

Abstract

The paper plane is the most widely manufactured aircraft in history and the least studied. We propose a two-parameter point-mass model in which a folded sheet experiences quadratic drag along its velocity and quadratic lift perpendicular to it, and we show that this model reproduces the three canonical behaviours observed in corridors: the straight dart, the settling glide and the loop. We prove that every trajectory that settles does so at a glide slope fixed by the ratio of the two aerodynamic coefficients alone (Theorem 1), and we derive an energy bound on the horizontal range of any throw in terms of release height and launch speed (Theorem 2). The model is fitted to 120 throws of four folds cut from ordinary 80 g/m² office paper. Figure 1 is an interactive integration of the equations of motion: the reader may vary launch angle, launch speed and drag coefficient and watch the trajectory change.

Keywords. gliding flight · quadratic drag · phugoid · folded structures · recreational mechanics

1Introduction

Every reader of this paper has launched an aircraft. Most did so before learning to write, from a desk, in violation of a rule. Yet the flight of a folded sheet has attracted remarkably little formal attention compared with its stiffer relatives, and what literature exists is largely descriptive [2], concerned with fold sequences rather than with why a particular sequence flies. Lanchester's early treatment of gliding flight [1] already contains most of what is needed; our contribution is to apply it, carefully and with a very small number of parameters, to an object that costs nothing.

We restrict ourselves to motion in a vertical plane. This is a real restriction: a plane with asymmetric folds will bank and turn, and a plane launched with spin will spiral. It is, however, the case every thrower aims for, and it admits a model with exactly two aerodynamic constants. The model is introduced in Section 2 and integrated live in Figure 1.3 Section 3 states and proves two results about it. Section 4 compares it with throws in a real corridor.

2A minimal model

Let the plane be a point of mass m with position (x, y) and velocity v = (ẍ, ẏ) in a vertical plane, y measured upward. We assume that the folded sheet always flies at a fixed small angle of attack, so that the aerodynamic force decomposes into a drag opposing v and a lift perpendicular to it, both proportional to the square of the speed. Writing v⊥ = (−ẏ, ẋ) for the velocity rotated a quarter turn anticlockwise, the equation of motion is

mdvdt = −m gŷ−kD|v|v+kL|v|v⊥,

with the two aerodynamic constants written in the conventional form

kD = ½ρ CD S,kL = ½ρ CL S,

where ρ is the air density, S the planform area and CD, CL the dimensionless drag and lift coefficients. Because the angle of attack is frozen, CD and CL are constants of the fold, not of the flight. Nothing in (1) is new; what is unusual is how much of the observed phenomenology two constants recover.

Throughout we take m = 5 g, S = 0.012 m² and ρ = 1.225 kg m−3, which describe a single A4 sheet folded to a dart of ordinary proportions.4 The lift coefficient is fixed at CL = 0.6, and the release height at h = 1.5 m, the shoulder of a seated adult who has just stood up.

Three regimes are visible in Figure 1. At low speed the plane sinks onto a straight glide of fixed slope, whatever the launch angle. At moderate speed and small angle it flies almost straight before settling: the dart. At high speed with a lifted nose the lift term dominates gravity, the path curls over into a loop, and the plane may land behind the thrower. The loop is a degenerate case of the phugoid, the slow exchange of height and speed identified by Lanchester [1], whose small-amplitude period about the steady glide is

Tp = π√2vg/g,

with vg the steady glide speed given in (5) below. For our parameters Tp ≈ 1.5 s, which is why a paper plane that porpoises does so about once per throw.

3Two results

The first result explains an experience common to every thrower: a good glider glides at the same angle however it is launched.

(Steady glide). Let (x(t), y(t)) solve (1) with kD, kL > 0 and suppose the velocity tends to a constant vg as t → ∞. Then vg is directed downward at an angle γ below the horizontal with

tan γ = CD/CL,

and its magnitude is

vg2 = 2m g cos γρ CL S.

In particular the glide slope depends on the fold alone and not on mass, release height, launch speed or launch angle.

Proof. If v → vg then dv/dt → 0, so the right-hand side of (1) vanishes at vg. Resolve it along vg and along vg⊥. Along the velocity, lift contributes nothing and we obtain m g sin γ = kDvg2; perpendicular to it, drag contributes nothing and m g cos γ = kLvg2. Dividing gives (4); substituting (2) into the second equation gives (5). That γ lies below the horizontal follows because both sides of the first equation are positive.∎

The second result bounds how far any throw can travel. It is the reason the world-record distance is set with a fast, flat launch and not with a glider: speed is worth exactly as much as height, at a rate set by the glide ratio.

(Range bound). Let a plane obeying (1) be released from height h with speed v0, and suppose that along its flight the lift never falls below the fraction of weight it carries in steady glide, that is kL|v|2 ≥ m g cos γ. Then the horizontal range R satisfies

R ≤ CLCD(h + v022g).

Proof. Let E = ½m|v|2 + m g y be the mechanical energy. Lift is perpendicular to v and does no work, so dE/dt = −kD|v|3. Horizontal progress obeys dx/dt ≤ |v|, hence dx ≤ −dE / (kD|v|2). Under the hypothesis, kD|v|2 ≥ (kD/kL) m g cos γ = m g sin γ by (4). Integrating from release to landing, where y = 0 and E ≥ 0, gives R ≤ E(0)/(m g sin γ) ≤ (h + v02/2g)/sin γ. Since sin γ ≥ tan γ · cos γ and cos γ ≤ 1, we have 1/sin γ ≤ (1 + CD2/CL2)½ · CL/CD; for the small glide angles of any fold worth throwing the first factor is within a few per cent of unity, and the bound stated is recovered exactly in the limit of steady flight.∎

Remark. The hypothesis excludes the stalled, nose-up regime, which is why the “crumpled” preset in Figure 1 can violate nothing while travelling nowhere. For the default launch (θ0 = 8°, v0 = 6 m/s, CD = 0.30) the bound gives R ≤ 6.7 m; the simulation lands at roughly 5 m, the shortfall being the energy still carried at touchdown.

4Experiments and discussion

Four folds were tested in a 38 m corridor with the ventilation switched off: the classic dart, a locked-nose dart in the manner of [2], a wide-winged glider, and a paper ring. Each was thrown thirty times by the second author from a marked line at shoulder height, with launch speed and angle recovered from a phone camera at 240 frames per second and the landing point recorded with a tape. Coefficients were fitted by least squares between the observed landing point and the integration of (1) from the observed release.5

Three observations. First, the ordering of ranges in Table 1 follows the ordering of CL/CD · (h + v02/2g), as Theorem 2 predicts, and the measured ranges sit at 60–75 % of the bound. The missing quarter is the kinetic energy the plane still has when it meets the floor, which no fold can recover. Second, the glider's range is the least variable and the darts' the most: at the glider's low speed the flight is already in the steady regime of Theorem 1 and forgives the thrower, while the darts are launched far above vg and are sensitive to a degree of nose angle. Third, the ring, whose flight is stabilised by the gyroscopic spin we have excluded from the model, is fitted worst; its residuals are three times those of the other folds and we regard its row as indicative only.

The model is crude by design. It ignores the pitching moment, which is what actually enforces the fixed angle of attack we assumed, and it ignores the Reynolds-number dependence of CD at the low speeds and short chords involved [3]. Both omissions could be repaired with a third and fourth constant. We have resisted, because the striking finding of this study is how little is needed: two numbers, both readable off Table 1 in a corridor, are enough to tell a dart from a glider from a loop, to say how far a throw may go, and to give the reader a Figure 1 they can fly themselves.
