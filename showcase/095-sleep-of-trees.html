I · Somnus plantarumThe oldest word for it

Linnaeus, who liked to name things before he could explain them, called it sleep. In a 1755 dissertation titled Somnus plantarum he catalogued the plants of the Uppsala garden that folded themselves shut after duskLinnaeus, Somnus plantarum (Uppsala, 1755), defended by his student Peter Bremer. Linnaeus was careful to say the analogy with animal sleep was an analogy. The word outlived the caution. — clover, wood sorrel, the beans in their rows — and noted that they did it whether or not the evening was cold, whether or not it was wet.

A century and a quarter later Charles Darwin and his son Francis devoted a long, patient chapter to the same behaviour, tying threads to leaflets and reading their positions by lamplight through the night. They named it nyctitropism and proposed a reason: a leaf held vertically at night presents its edge to the open sky and loses less heat by radiation.C. & F. Darwin, The Power of Movement in Plants (1880). Their frost experiments were crude — leaves pinned open froze more readily than leaves left to fold — but the direction of the effect has held up. They were partly right, which in this field counts as a triumph.

What neither could ask is the question that a modern instrument answers almost casually: not whether leaves move at night, but whether the whole tree does — trunk, limbs, the entire standing architecture — and by how much.

II · The clockA clock with no face

In 1729 the astronomer Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan shut a Mimosa pudica in a dark cupboard and looked in on it at intervals. The leaves went on opening in the morning and closing at night, in a cupboard with no morning and no night.De Mairan never wrote it up. Marchant reported the observation to the Académie Royale des Sciences in a single paragraph — arguably the founding document of chronobiology, and about a hundred words long. The rhythm was not a response to the sun. It was the plant's own.

We can now name the machinery. A small set of genes suppress one another in a loop that takes roughly twenty-four hours to come back around; light and temperature nudge the loop into step with the local day. The point of such a clock is anticipation. A tree that begins opening its stomata before the sun clears the ridge is already trading water for carbon while a merely reactive tree is still waiting to be told.Anticipation is worth real sugar. Plants whose internal period is experimentally mismatched to their light cycle fix less carbon and grow measurably less — the clock earns its keep.

A tree at night is not switched off. It is running a different program.— the working assumption of this essay

III · The measurementTen centimetres of descent

In 2016 a group of surveyors and plant scientists parked a terrestrial laser scanner beside a silver birch and left it running all night.Terrestrial laser scanning sweeps a pulsed beam across a scene and times the return, building a cloud of millions of points at millimetre precision. The same instrument surveys quarries, cathedral vaults and glaciers. Nothing touches the tree. They did it twice: once in Finland, once in Austria, both times near an equinox, both times on a night with almost no wind — because wind is the noise that has always drowned this signal.

The scans, taken every ten minutes, showed the crown sinking. Branches and leaf tips of a five-metre birch dropped as much as ten centimetres over the course of the night, reached their lowest point an hour or two before sunrise, and returned to their daytime position within a few hours of first light.Puttonen and colleagues, Frontiers in Plant Science 7:222 (2016). Two trees, two countries, the same shape of curve — which is what turned a curiosity into a result. It was not the temperature, which kept falling after the tree had already started to rise. It was not the wind, because there wasn't any.

A later survey scanned twenty-two species through the night and found that the descent is not one movement but many. Some crowns dip once and recover; others oscillate on a shorter period, hours long, as though the tree were breathing.Zlinszky & Barfod, Plant Signaling & Behavior (2018). Sub-circadian oscillation in tree water status is contested, and worth watching.

IV · The mechanismThe pump shuts off

A tree does not lift water. It is drained upward. Evaporation at the leaf surface pulls on a continuous thread of water that runs unbroken from the stomata to the root hairs, and the whole tree hangs, faintly, from that tension. Every cell along the way is pressed firm against its own wall by the water inside it.Turgor: the hydrostatic pressure of the cell contents against the cell wall. A wilting basil plant on a windowsill is the same physics, staged at a scale you can watch in an afternoon.

At dusk the stomata close. The pull relaxes; the tissue softens by a fraction of a per cent; and the branch, which was never rigid, does the only thing an unsupported cantilever can do. It obeys gravity. The descent is not an action. It is the absence of one.

Some plants do it properly, with a hinge. Legumes carry swollen motor organs called pulvini at the base of each leaflet, and drive potassium ions across their membranes to shift water from one side of the hinge to the other, folding the leaf like a hand.The pulvinus is a genuine actuator: ions out, water follows, that flank goes slack, the leaf folds. Birches have nothing of the kind, which is what makes their nightly slump so eloquent. A birch has no such organ. Its descent is a whole body going slack.

V · Before lightWhat returns before the sun

The detail that keeps the story from being purely mechanical is the timing of the recovery. In several of the scanned records the crown starts back up before there is any light to explain it — the tree refilling on schedule, on the strength of its own clock, in the last dark hour.

It is worth being careful with the word we started with. Trees do not sleep the way an animal sleeps: no neurons, no consolidation, nothing being replayed. What the scans show is smaller and stranger than sleep and easier to verify — a body that keeps time, relaxes on a schedule, and gathers itself again before the morning arrives to require it.

Stand under a birch two hours before dawn, at the bottom of its night, and the crown is measurably closer to you than it was at noon. You will not notice. The instruments do, and that is enough of a reason to go out and stand there.
