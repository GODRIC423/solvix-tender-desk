Notes onmotion

Kinetic typography is usually a film technique: words timed to a voice, cut to a beat, exported once and watched the same way by everyone. This page moves the timeline into your hand. There is no autoplay. The scrollbar is the playhead, and its velocity is the only performer. Read slowly and the page is a poster. Read quickly and the poster starts to tear.

The mechanics are deliberately plain. On every frame the script measures how far the page travelled since the last one, smooths that number, clamps it, and writes it into two CSS custom properties: a signed velocity and its absolute value. Every deformation you see is a single CSS transform reading one of those properties. Skew reads the sign. Stretch reads the magnitude. The split statement multiplies velocity by each letter’s distance from the centre of its line, so the outer letters travel farthest and the middle barely stirs. The marquee keeps its own position and simply flips direction whenever your scroll direction flips, then borrows your speed on top of its idle drift.

Two typefaces do all the work. Six Caps is an ultra-condensed grotesque with almost no counters, which means it survives being sheared and stretched without turning to mush. Anton carries the captions: heavy enough to sit next to a wall of display type without disappearing, quiet enough to be read. The palette is white, black and one red, and the red is rationed: a slash in the counters, a rule between rows, a single full stop at the end.

  1. Velocity, not positionThe page does not care where you are, only how fast you got there. Parallax measures place; this measures effort.
  2. Every effect is reversibleStop, and within a second every glyph is back in its resting form. Nothing is broken, only bent.
  3. One redColour is the loudest instrument, so it gets one note per screen.
  4. Type is the only imageNo photographs, no icons, no illustrations. If it cannot be said in letters it is not on the page.
