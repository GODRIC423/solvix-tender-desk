Ravenna, Emilia-Romagna

The city that kept its light in glass

Rome fell, and Ravenna answered by setting several million pieces of coloured glass into wet lime. Fifteen centuries later, the walls still move when you do.

MarginaliaRavenna was the capital of the Western Empire from 402, then of Theodoric's Ostrogothic kingdom, then of Byzantine Italy. Each ruler left a ceiling.

A small city, three empires, one medium

Nothing about Ravenna prepares you for its interiors. From the street the churches are plain brick, the colour of dry terracotta, with small windows and no sculpture to speak of. Then a door opens onto a room made entirely of light. The trick is a simple one, repeated with fanatical patience: tesserae, cubes of glass and stone rarely larger than a fingernail, pressed one at a time into a bed of lime that was still soft, each one set at a slightly different angle from its neighbour.

That deliberate irregularity is the whole secret. A wall of perfectly flat tiles would behave like a mirror and go dead from most positions. Ravenna's walls are never flat. Every tessera tilts, and so every step you take across the nave brings a different constellation of them into the light. The mosaic is not a picture you look at but a surface that looks back, and it changes its mind as you pass.

TechniqueGold tesserae are a sandwich: a sheet of beaten gold leaf laid on a glass base, then sealed under a second, wafer-thin layer called the cartellina.

How gold is made to stand still

The gold in these vaults is real, and there is a great deal of it, but it is thinner than breath. A leaf of gold was laid onto a slab of glass, covered with another sheet of molten glass barely a millimetre thick, and fused. The result could be cut into cubes that would never tarnish, because the metal was never exposed to air. Then the mosaicist did something counter-intuitive: rather than laying the gold ground smooth, he tipped the cubes forward, toward the floor, so that they would catch the lamps below and throw the light down onto the faces of the congregation.

Look closely at the gold in San Vitale and you will see that it is not one colour. Some cubes were laid over red glass, some over green, some over a warm amber, and the tint bleeds through the leaf. In the shadows the gold cools toward olive; in direct light it flares almost white. The blue behind the figures is a smalto coloured with cobalt, and the deepest of it, the night sky of the Galla Placidia vault, reads as lapis lazuli even though no lapis was used. It is glass pretending to be a stone, and the pretence is perfect.

ItineraryEight monuments in Ravenna were inscribed together on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1996. These three are the ones people return to.

Three rooms to stand in

The Mausoleum of Galla Placidia is the smallest and the oldest, a cross-shaped brick box built around 425 for an empress who was, in the end, buried in Rome. Inside, the vault is a field of gold stars on a blue that deepens as your eyes adjust, more than five hundred of them, and above the door the Good Shepherd sits among his sheep in a landscape that still remembers Roman naturalism. It is the last time in Ravenna that grass looks like grass.

Sant'Apollinare Nuovo, begun under the Ostrogoth Theodoric around 505, is a long basilica whose nave walls carry two processions: twenty-two virgin martyrs on one side, twenty-six male martyrs on the other, each robed in white and gold, each holding a crown, walking in step toward the apse. The rhythm of the repeating figures against the gold is one of the great inventions of Western art, and the palm trees between them are set in shades of green that no painter of the period could have matched.

San Vitale, consecrated in 547 under the Byzantine emperor Justinian, is the summit. The presbytery is a cage of colour, and on its two side walls stand the imperial panels: Justinian with his bishop Maximian and his guards, Theodora with her ladies, all of them looking straight out at you across a gold ground, all of them shod in purple. Neither emperor nor empress ever set foot in the city. They did not need to. They had themselves laid into its walls.

GroutThe dark lines between tiles are not a flaw to be minimised. Byzantine mosaicists widened them deliberately in the backgrounds so that the gold would read as a shimmer rather than a sheet.

In praise of the gap

Modern eyes, trained by screens, read the joints between tesserae as noise. The mosaicists read them as rhythm. In Ravenna the grout is nearly always a warm dark grey, the natural colour of the lime once it has taken up dust and smoke, and it does exactly what a leading does in a stained-glass window: it holds each colour apart so that none of them can muddy another. Stand close and the picture dissolves into a swarm of separate cubes. Step back three paces and the swarm resolves, not into a smooth image, but into something more alive than smooth, a picture that seems to be breathing.

The canvas at the top of this page tries to honour that. Each tile is cut a little wrong. The grout is never hidden. The gold does not glow evenly but in a passing band, the way a lamp carried down a nave would light one bay and then the next. Type a word and it is set the way the letters over Justinian's head were set, in gold on blue, with a single course of terracotta to hold the edge. It is a toy. But it is a toy built out of the right idea.
