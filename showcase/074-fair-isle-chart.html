Working notes

Note oneHow a band is built

Fair Isle is a two-colour trade. Each round carries one ground shade and one pattern shade; the resting yarn floats along the back of the work, caught every few stitches so that a finger cannot snag it. That discipline is why a good band refuses to be busy — paint a third shade into a row and this sheet flags it in the left margin. It is a nudge, not a law. Shetland knitters have been quietly breaking it for two hundred years.

Note twoReading the sheet

Charts are read the way fabric grows: from the bottom row upward, and from right to left, the direction stitches leave the left needle. Row numbers climb the right-hand margin, odd rows in bold, because those are the ones you count aloud. One square is one stitch. The eleven-stitch repeat is marked by the heavier rules every fifth column, so you can check a motif is landing where it should before you commit forty rounds of wool to it.

Note threeOn shading

The gradation in the default band — yarrow green at the points, madder through the shoulders, gorse at the heart — is the oldest trick in the drawer. Hold the ground steady, move the pattern shade one step every pair of rows, and a flat motif begins to glow from the inside. Change any square and the ledger recounts itself: stitches, share of the round, and metres of wool at our own dyed-in-the-fleece gauge.
