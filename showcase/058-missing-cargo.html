The Case of theMissing Cargo

I

The Rain Came First

Chapter oneOffice, 21:15Dunmore Street
I. The rain came first

The rain came before the client did. It always does in this town. She walked into my office at a quarter past nine with an umbrella she hadn't opened and a manifest she hadn't read. Forty feet of steel, she said. Container TGHU 402318-7. It had come off the Meridian Star on the fourth, scanned into Bay 19 at 02:14, and by the morning shift it was gone.

No truck. No crane log. No gate ticket. Just a rectangle of dry concrete where a box should have been, the only dry thing in the whole yard. I asked her what was inside. She said that was the second thing she was paying me to find out. I told her my rate. She didn't blink, which in my experience is the most expensive thing a client can do.

II

Bay Nineteen

Chapter twoTerminal, 03:40Wet end
II. Bay nineteen

Bay 19 sits at the wet end of the terminal, where the floodlights give up and the sea starts making suggestions. I spent the night there with a thermos and a borrowed hi-vis vest, counting containers the way other men count sheep. The gate logs were clean. That was what bothered me. Nothing is clean at the Port of Halvard; the gulls have records.

At 03:40 a reach stacker rolled past with its lights off and a cab empty of anyone who wanted to be seen. I wrote down the plate by the glow of my cigarette. It belonged to a machine the yard had listed as out of service since August. Somebody was moving steel with a ghost, and ghosts don't fill in timesheets.

III

The Manifest

Chapter threeOffice, 11:00Under the blinds
III. The manifest

Paper lies more politely than people. The bill of lading said industrial fasteners, 18,400 kilograms. The customs seal number had been re-typed with a different ribbon; you could see it if you tilted the page. And the weighbridge ticket, the one thing nobody thinks to check, put the box at 26,100 on arrival. Seven tonnes of something that was not bolts.

I laid the documents out on the desk and let the blinds cut them into stripes. Every lie has a seam. This one ran straight through the shipper's signature, which had been made by a man who signs a lot of things and reads very few of them.

IV

The Man in the Weigh House

Chapter fourWeighbridge, 17:50Twenty-two years
IV. The man in the weigh house

His name was Deverell and he had worked the weighbridge for twenty-two years. He wasn't hard to find and he wasn't hard to break; men who keep honest numbers for two decades never learn how to carry a crooked one. He'd been paid to look at the ceiling for eleven minutes. He told me the ceiling had a water stain shaped like Portugal. He'd never noticed before.

The container went out on the ghost stacker, onto a flatbed with agricultural plates, and north on the coast road towards Merrow. He remembered the truck because the driver had been whistling. Nobody whistles at four in the morning unless they have already been paid.

V

Dry Concrete

Chapter fiveBarn, 06:30Outside Merrow
V. Dry concrete

I found it in a barn two miles past Merrow, doors open, seals cut, seven tonnes lighter than when it arrived. Whatever had ridden inside was gone, and the fasteners had been left behind like a punchline: eighteen thousand kilograms of bolts nobody had ever meant to sell. I sat on a crate and smoked one to the filter.

I called the client from a phone box in the rain. She didn't sound surprised, which told me the last thing I needed to know and the one thing I hadn't been paid to. Some cases you close. Some you just stop opening. I typed up the report, billed her for the thermos, and watched the blinds cut the morning into pieces on the office floor.

Case Closed File 0417 · Unresolved

Container TGHU 402318-7 was returned to the terminal on the ninth, empty, and re-entered the yard inventory as if it had never left. Deverell took early retirement. The flatbed with the agricultural plates has not been seen on the coast road since.

The client paid in cash. The rain did not stop.
