The Meridian · Southern Party Notebook III · Cape Halloran to the Pole Lat. 77° 51′ S → 90° 00′ S

Expedition log · Nov. 1911 – Jan. 1912

Antarctica1911

Ten entries copied fair from the sledging notebooks of Commander Aldous Fenwick, who led five men, eight ponies and a motor sledge south from Cape Halloran across the Barrier, up the Corvin Glacier and onto the plateau. Noon temperatures and the day's run are given as he recorded them.

The Southern Journey

10 entries · 811 statute miles · 78 days out
  1. No.01

    −14 °C · 7 °FDay 1Wind SSE, light

    Wednesday, 1 November 1911

    Departure from Cape Halloran

    Away at last at 10.40 with the whole shore party lined up on the sea ice, hats off, nobody saying much. The ponies pulled well over the first miles of Barrier; Halvorsen swears they know where they are going. The motor sledge ran ahead making a great noise and a small distance.

    Camped nine miles out under a sky the colour of pewter. The hut is a black speck behind us. Marlow read the thermometer aloud and then wrote it down twice, as if it might be argued with.

    9 mi today · 9 mi from base

    Barrier surface hard sastrugi at first, then good going. One ration bag found split — sewn by Brandt at supper.

  2. No.02

    −19 °C · −2 °FDay 6Wind S, force 4

    Monday, 6 November 1911

    The motor is left behind

    The big cylinder cracked at noon with a sound like a rifle shot and that is the end of the motor sledge. Okafor spent two hours on his back under it with a blowlamp and came out swearing in three languages. We took the good sledge-meter off it and left the rest as a landmark for the return.

    Its load is now divided among the ponies, who feel the difference. Eleven miles, hard won. I am not sorry to be rid of the noise.

    11 mi today · 58 mi from base

    Okafor asks that the engine's failure be recorded as "the cold's fault and not his." So recorded.

  3. No.03

    −23 °C · −9 °FDay 14Blizzard, S, force 9

    Tuesday, 14 November 1911

    Third day lying up

    Nothing to be done. The tent walls thunder and the drift piles against the weather side so that the inside of the canvas is pressed against our faces when we sleep. We have shortened the ration by a third and nobody has remarked on it, which is worse than if they had.

    Halvorsen goes out each hour to the ponies and comes back with his beard iced solid, reporting that they stand with their backs to it and endure. Zero miles. The thermometer says minus twenty-three; the wind says otherwise.

    0 mi today · 131 mi from base

    Marlow reads Browning aloud to the tent. Brandt requests "anything with a ship in it" instead.

  4. No.04

    −21 °C · −6 °FDay 23Wind SE, force 3

    Thursday, 23 November 1911

    Depot Charlotte is laid

    We built the depot at noon: a cairn eight feet high with a black flag on a bamboo, and a fortnight's food and oil for five men buried beneath it. Brandt named it for his daughter, and no one thought to name it anything else. I took the bearings three times.

    The going is soft now and the ponies sink to the hock. Thirteen miles, which I had not dared hope for. Tonight the sun went all the way round the sky without setting and the Barrier turned a faint rose colour, then blue.

    13 mi today · 236 mi from base

    Depot: 4 tins biscuit, 2 tins pemmican, 3 gal. oil, 1 spare primus. Marked by cairn & flag, bearing 187° from the last camp.

  5. No.05

    −12 °C · 10 °FDay 33Calm

    Sunday, 3 December 1911

    The foot of the Corvin Glacier

    Warm — absurdly warm, minus twelve at noon with the sun on the snow so that we marched in shirtsleeves and burnt our lips. Ahead the glacier comes down between the mountains like a river of broken glass, fifteen miles across at its mouth and tilted up toward a sky we cannot see the end of.

    Fifteen miles today, our best. The ponies are done, and know it. Tomorrow we shall do what has to be done and I will not write about it.

    15 mi today · 349 mi from base

    Aneroid: 210 ft above Barrier level. Mountains to the E named provisionally the Okafor Range — he objects, but not strongly.

  6. No.06

    −17 °C · 1 °FDay 42Wind down-glacier, force 5

    Tuesday, 12 December 1911

    Man-hauling on the glacier

    Five men in harness now, two hundred pounds apiece behind us, and the surface a maze of pressure ridges and blue ice that the crampons will not bite. Twice the leading sledge went into a crevasse to its crossbar and twice we hauled it out by main force, lying flat and swearing quietly.

    Eight miles, and every one of them earned. Brandt has a frostbitten heel and says nothing about it; Marlow found out anyway and dressed it. The glacier climbs and climbs.

    8 mi today · 421 mi from base

    Sledge No. 2 runner cracked and lashed with rawhide. Rations: full again since the ponies' feed came off the loads.

  7. No.07

    −28 °C · −18 °FDay 51Wind S, force 4

    Thursday, 21 December 1911

    The head of the glacier, 8,900 feet

    Midsummer's day, and we have reached the top of the glacier. The mountains fall away on either hand and ahead there is only a white plain running to the horizon with nothing on it at all — not a rock, not a shadow, not a mark. I have never seen anything so empty or so beautiful. Okafor stood a long time and then said, "Well, there it is," which is as good as anything.

    The cold has a different quality up here, thin and sharp; the breath freezes in the moustache in seconds. Twelve miles.

    12 mi today · 517 mi from base

    Upper Glacier Depot laid: 1 week's food for 5. Christmas pudding kept back by Marlow, who denies it.

  8. No.08

    −31 °C · −24 °FDay 60Wind SSW, force 6

    Saturday, 30 December 1911

    The supporting party turns back

    Halvorsen and Brandt turned for home this morning with the light sledge and a fortnight's food. We shook hands all round and then, because that seemed inadequate, did it again. They went north and we went south and within an hour there was nothing to see of them but the sledge tracks, and then not those.

    Three of us now: Marlow, Okafor and myself. Fourteen miles over a surface like coarse sand. The plateau has a wind that never stops and never varies, and I am beginning to hate it personally.

    14 mi today · 618 mi from base

    Brandt's heel worsening — the chief reason for sending him back, though he was told it was the rations.

  9. No.09

    −36 °C · −33 °FDay 70Wind S, force 5

    Tuesday, 9 January 1912

    Past the farthest south

    At noon we passed the latitude of Marlow's own expedition of 1908, and he had the grace to say nothing about it beyond "Good," and to shake my hand. The thermometer stood at minus thirty-six. The oil is running short because the cold makes the primus greedy, and we have cut the hot lunch.

    Sixteen miles, our best on the plateau. We are all three lighter than we were and Okafor's nose has gone the colour of a plum. Ninety miles to go, by the sledge-meter and the sun.

    16 mi today · 749 mi from base

    Sun obs. at noon: 88° 25′ S. Sastrugi running SE–NW, very hard. Skis useless; carried.

  10. No.10

    −33 °C · −27 °FDay 78Wind S, force 3

    Wednesday, 17 January 1912

    The Pole

    We have reached it. By our observations we are within a mile of the southern extremity of the earth, and there is nothing here — no cairn, no flag, no sign that any man has stood on this place before us. We built our own cairn and flew the flag from a ski, and Marlow took the photograph with hands that would barely work the shutter.

    It is a bleak place; I will not pretend otherwise. Minus thirty-three, a small wind, and eight hundred and eleven miles between us and the hut. Tomorrow we turn north. I have written the names of all five men on a slip of paper and left it in a tin beneath the stones.

    14 mi today · 811 mi from base

    Position by three sights: 89° 59′ S. Left in the cairn: names, the date, one tin of biscuit, and the Meridian's pennant.
