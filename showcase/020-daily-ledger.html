Ten Thousand Cross on Foot Before Breakfast

Harbor Span Opens at Dawn; Ferrymen Sound Last Whistle

Mayor Cuts Ribbon in Fog as Crowd Surges Past Barriers — Toll Fixed at Two Cents, Free for Schoolchildren — Twelve Years and Nine Million Dollars in the Building

By Our Staff Correspondent

The Harbor Span, argued over in three administrations and built through two hard winters, carried its first paying traffic at 5:52 o'clock this morning when a milkman named Otto Brenner drove a two-horse wagon across from the Northside approach and refused, on principle, to be photographed.

Behind him came the city. Police estimated that ten thousand persons crossed on foot in the first two hours, most of them simply to say they had done it and walk back. Mothers pushed perambulators. A brass band from the Ironworkers' Local marched to the center pier, played "Hail, Columbia" twice, and was asked by a sergeant to disperse so that streetcars might pass.

Mayor Cassius Redfern cut the ribbon with a pair of shears borrowed at the last moment from a tailor in the crowd, the ceremonial pair having been left in a motor-car at City Hall. "This bridge," he said, "was promised to your fathers. It is delivered to you." The remark was received well, though the fog swallowed most of it.

The Ferries. As the Mayor spoke, the four boats of the Harbor Ferry Company lay off the old slip with steam up and flags at the staff. At six o'clock precisely each sounded three long blasts, the signal for a final crossing. The company, which has run the route since 1871, will discontinue passenger service at midnight Saturday. Its 140 employees have been offered posts with the Bridge Authority.

The toll, two cents for foot passengers and fifteen for motor-cars, was fixed by the Authority on Tuesday over the protest of the Automobile Club. Children in school uniform pass free until 9 o'clock. Collectors in new gray coats reported takings of $212.40 for the first hour, a figure the Authority's comptroller called "gratifying" and the Club's counsel called "an ambush."

Engineers pronounced the structure sound after the morning's load, the center span having deflected four inches under the crowd and returned. Chief Engineer Margarethe Lund, who has slept in a shed on the Southside pier for the better part of a year, was found at seven o'clock eating an egg sandwich on the parapet and declined all comment except "It holds."

Ocean Flight

Aviatrix Lands at Lisbon After Thirty-One Hours Aloft

Miss Ida Rowan Flies Alone From Halifax in Borrowed Monoplane; Compass Failed Over Azores, She Steered by Stars

Lisbon, by cable. — Miss Ida Rowan of this city, twenty-six years old and a schoolteacher by profession, brought the monoplane Tern down on the military field at Alverca at 4:10 o'clock this afternoon, thirty-one hours and eight minutes after leaving Halifax, and asked first for a glass of water and second for a telegraph blank.

She had flown the last eleven hours without a compass. The instrument, she said, "began to wander" somewhere south of the Azores, and she held her course thereafter by the Pole Star and, after dawn, by the sun on her left cheek. She carried four sandwiches and ate two.

The Tern belongs to the Atlantic Aero Company, which lent it on the understanding that Miss Rowan would not attempt the crossing until spring. She left a note. Officials of the company, reached at their homes, said they were "reviewing the correspondence" and then that they were "very proud."

Her mother, Mrs. Agnes Rowan of Linden Street, learned of the landing from a neighbor with a wireless set and said only, "She always did finish her sums."

Labor

Rail Strike Averted at Midnight Hour

Brotherhoods Accept Six-Cent Rise and Eight-Hour Day; Freight Moves at Dawn After Twelve Days of Uncertainty

A strike that would have stopped every wheel on the Meridian and Northern lines at one minute past midnight was called off at eleven fifty-two, when a federal mediator carried a single typewritten sheet from the Brotherhoods' rooms on the fourth floor of the Hotel Palladian to the railroad's suite on the sixth, and back again with a signature on it.

The sheet grants the trainmen six cents an hour and fixes the working day at eight hours with time and a half thereafter. It gives the company two years of peace and a promise that the yards at Junction City will be manned through the grain rush. Neither side would call it a victory. Both sides sent for coffee.

The membership ratified the pact by telegraph vote of 4,116 to 388 before dawn. The first eastbound freight, forty cars of wheat and eleven of live hogs, left Junction City at 5:40 o'clock, its crew receiving the new wage from the moment they climbed aboard.

Grain merchants, who had chartered barges against the stoppage, are now trying to sell them.

Science

Astronomers Report New Comet in Morning Sky

Discovered by Night Watchman With Home-Made Telescope; Will Brighten Through the Month, Observatory Says

The Hillcrest Observatory confirmed last night that a comet, now designated 1936f and to be known as Comet Halvorsen, is approaching the sun and should be plainly visible to the naked eye in the eastern sky an hour before dawn for the next three weeks.

It was found not by the Observatory but by Mr. Nils Halvorsen, sixty-one, night watchman at the Standard Newsprint mill, who has spent his lunch hours for eleven years at a six-inch reflector he ground himself in a cellar. He saw the object on Sunday, noted its position on a scrap of wrapping paper, and telephoned the Observatory on Monday from the mill office, reversing the charges.

Dr. Eleanor Voss, who confirmed the sighting, called it "a very respectable comet" and the discoverer "a very patient man." The tail should reach a length of eight degrees by the fifteenth. Mr. Halvorsen has asked that the mill not change his shift.

Finance

Market Rallies on Grain News; Wheat Up 3⅞

Winnipeg Cable Reports Frost in the Prairies — Bridge Bonds Lead Advance — Ferry Shares Collapse to 12½ in Heavy Selling

Wheat led the exchanges upward from the opening gong on a cable from Winnipeg reporting killing frost across the northern prairies, and the whole list followed with a briskness not seen since spring. Northern Grain & Elevator closed at 58⅞, a gain of 3⅞, on volume the floor described as "hysterical."

The Bridge Authority's 4s, which opened at par on Monday to polite indifference, changed hands at 101⅛ by the close as it became evident that the span would in fact carry traffic and in fact collect for it. Bankers who had underwritten the issue at a discount were seen lunching well.

The day's casualty was Consolidated Ferry preferred, which fell 4¼ to 12½ on sales that began at the opening and did not stop. The company will pay its September dividend, a director said, "if only for form's sake."

Call money held at 1 per cent. Sterling was steady. The Exchange will observe its usual hours to-morrow.

The City

Old Clock Tower to Chime Again After Forty Years

Four Bells Recast in Their Own Metal; First Test at Six To-night — Residents of Cathedral Square Advised to Remove Cats From Sills

The bells of the Custom House clock tower, silent since a crack in the tenor was discovered in 1896 and the whole peal condemned as a hazard to the roof, will ring the hour again at six o'clock this evening, if a test this afternoon satisfies the Commissioner of Buildings.

The four bells were lowered in February, broken up, and recast in their own bronze by the Whitlock foundry at a cost of $4,300 raised by subscription, two dollars at a time, through this newspaper. The tenor weighs 1,900 pounds and is tuned, the founder says, "as close to the old note as anyone living can remember it."

The clock itself, a Bostock movement of 1858, was cleaned and found to be running four minutes fast, which the Custom House staff say explains a great deal about the last forty years.

Cathedral Square shopkeepers have been notified. Mrs. Pauline Herrick, who has lived opposite since 1901, said she intended to sit on her steps and listen "with both ears, for once."
