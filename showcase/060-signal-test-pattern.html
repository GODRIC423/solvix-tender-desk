reading the bars

seven bars, one reverse strip, and a pluge

The picture on channel 1 is the SMPTE colour bar signal, standardised in 1978 as engineering guideline EG 1. Nothing about it is decorative. Every rectangle exists so that an engineer with a waveform monitor and a vectorscope can prove the signal chain is honest.

The top two thirds carry the seven bars at 75 percent amplitude: grey, yellow, cyan, green, magenta, red, blue. In that order the luminance falls step by step, which is why the bars read as a staircase on a waveform monitor. On a vectorscope the six coloured bars land on six little target boxes; if a dot sits outside its box, the chroma phase or gain is wrong.

The thin middle strip flips the order into blue, black, magenta, black, cyan, black, grey. Turn the chroma all the way down with a blue-only filter and the columns above and below either match or they don't. That is the whole test, and it takes about two seconds.

The bottom row is the part most people never notice. The dark navy is −I, the purple is +Q: the two colour-difference axes of NTSC, present so demodulator phase can be checked. Then 100 percent white, then black, and finally the PLUGE, the Picture Line-Up Generating Equipment pattern, three slivers at 3.5 percent below black, black, and 3.5 percent above. Set brightness so the left sliver disappears and the right one barely shows, and your black level is correct. Every monitor in a broadcast plant was lined up against those three slivers.

  • 75% bars

    Descending luminance. Checks gain, chroma phase and saturation on the vectorscope targets.

  • reverse bars

    Viewed through a blue filter the strip should match the bars above it. Mismatch means hue or chroma error.

  • −i · white · +q · pluge

    Colour-difference axes, reference white, and three near-black slivers for setting brightness.

  • 1 khz tone

    Bars are almost always sent with a 1 kHz sine at reference level so the audio path can be aligned at the same time.

reference level · sine
1 000 hz

The tone you can switch on above is a pure sine wave at one kilohertz, generated in your browser with an oscillator and a gain stage that ramps up over a few milliseconds so it never clicks. Broadcasters chose 1 kHz because it sits in the middle of the ear's most sensitive range, it is easy to read on a meter, and any hum, distortion or dropout in the chain is obvious against it.

the sound of nothing

why a tone came with the picture

Before a network feed arrived, a studio sent bars and tone down the line for a few minutes. The receiving engineer checked the bars on the scopes and set the audio meter to read zero on the tone. If the picture was right and the tone read 0 dBu, whatever followed would be delivered as intended. If the level was off by 6 dB, the whole programme would be too loud or too quiet, so you fixed it before it mattered.

The tone also had a second job. A carrier with no sound on it and no picture on it looks, to automated monitoring, exactly like a dead transmitter. Bars and tone told the machines, and anyone flicking past, that the station was alive and simply had nothing to say yet.

Degaussing is a different fix for a different fault. Colour tubes have a steel shadow mask just behind the glass, and any stray magnetism in it bends the electron beams so the colours smear toward the corners. A coil around the tube fires a decaying alternating field on power-up to scrub the mask clean. It thumps, the picture heaves and rainbows for a second, then settles. Press the red button, or tap the screen, to see the effect.

a short history

the pictures television showed when nothing was on

  1. 1939

    the indian head test pattern

    RCA's monochrome card, drawn by Brooks Marsh, mixed a portrait with resolution wedges, a grey scale and circles for checking geometry. American stations ran it before sign-on and after sign-off for three decades. It was designed to be stared at by engineers, and it was.

  2. 1967

    bbc test card f

    The BBC's first colour test card, photographed rather than drawn: a girl playing noughts and crosses with a clown doll inside a crosshatch of gratings and grey steps. Britain looked at it for so many hours that the model, Carole Hersee, became one of the most broadcast faces in history.

  3. 1968

    philips pm5544

    Erik Helmer Nielsen's electronic pattern, generated by a box instead of a camera pointed at cardboard. A circle to prove linearity, a crosshatch for convergence, colour bars, a grey scale, frequency gratings and a space in the middle for the station name and clock. Channel 2 above is a tribute to it. Dozens of countries adopted it.

  4. 1978

    smpte eg 1 colour bars

    The Society of Motion Picture and Television Engineers formalised the bar pattern seen on channel 1: 75 percent bars, a reverse strip and the PLUGE. It became the universal way to line up NTSC equipment, and its silhouette still means "technical difficulties" to anyone who grew up with a television.

  5. 2002

    arib std-b28 hd bars

    For high definition, Japan's ARIB revised the bars with a 40 percent grey surround, 100 percent and 75 percent bar rows and a luminance ramp. It is what most HD cameras generate today when you switch them to bars. The 1 kHz tone survived the transition untouched.

  6. now

    what remains

    Stations no longer sign off, so the test card has mostly retired to the rack room, the camera menu, and the memory of anyone who fell asleep with the television on. The tone is still 1 kHz. The bars are still seven. A degauss coil still thumps in the few tubes left running.
