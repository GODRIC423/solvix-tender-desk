A lowland rainforest is not one habitat but four, stacked. Each floor has its own light budget, its own weather and its own tenants. Scroll to cut the shadow box open and descend.

01 Emergent · the wind floor

Above the roof

45 – 65 metres

A handful of giants push clear of the canopy and stand alone in the weather. A kapok can hold sixty-five metres of trunk upright on buttress roots the width of a doorway, and its crown pays for the view: unfiltered sun, a twenty-degree swing between noon and midnight, and gusts that never reach the layer below.

Almost nothing lives up here permanently. The residents are travellers — raptors that hunt by dropping, butterflies riding the thermals between crowns, and seeds engineered as gliders and helicopters, because at this height there is nothing left to catch them.

Who you meet up here

  • Adds two to four metres a year in the race for light, then spreads buttress roots ten metres wide to stay standing in soil barely deeper than a bathtub.

  • Talons as long as a grizzly's claws. It perches on an emergent branch, watches the canopy roof beneath it, and takes sloths and monkeys off the treetops by falling on them.

  • Rides warm air between crowns. The blue is not pigment at all but layered scales that interfere with light — a paper trick, done in keratin.

  • Only long-tongued orchid bees can open its flowers, and only the agouti can crack the fruit. Remove either one and a 50-metre tree quietly stops reproducing.

02 Canopy · the green roof

A second ground

20 – 45 metres

Roughly eight in ten of the forest's living things are up here, on a surface that behaves like ground: continuous, walkable for anything with hands or claws, and carrying a soil of its own. Litter and rain collect in the crooks of branches until epiphytes take root in mid-air.

A single tank bromeliad can hold eight litres of water thirty metres above the earth. Tree frogs breed in it; crabs, salamanders and mosquito larvae complete whole life cycles in it and never touch the floor. The roof also takes the weather first — most of the rain and nine tenths of the light stop here.

Tenants of the roof

  • An enlarged hyoid bone turns the throat into a resonating box. The dawn chorus carries five kilometres — loud enough to map troop boundaries without anyone moving.

  • Moves about forty metres a day and grows algae in grooved hair for camouflage. It descends to the forest floor roughly once a week, and only to defecate.

  • The bill is honeycombed keratin around air — light enough to fly with, and used as a radiator: the bird dumps body heat through it and tucks it under a wing to sleep.

  • Not a parasite — it only uses the branch as a perch, drinking from the rosette it forms and feeding on the litter that falls into it.

03 Understory · the dim room

Waiting for a gap

5 – 20 metres

Below the roof the air goes still and heavy — near-saturated, a few degrees cooler, almost windless. Two to five per cent of the sunlight reaches this far, and it arrives as brief moving coins of light that slide across the leaf litter and vanish.

Plants answer by going wide instead of tall. Leaves broaden into paddles a metre across, and many finish in a drawn-out drip tip so water sheets off before moss and fungus can colonise the surface. Saplings hold position here for decades, barely growing, waiting for a canopy tree to fall and tear open a patch of sky they can race into.

In the half-light

  • The only cat that regularly kills by biting through the skull. It works all four layers — swims the rivers, rests on understory branches, hunts on the floor.

  • The toxin is borrowed, not made: it comes from the mites and ants they eat. Raised on crickets in captivity, they are entirely harmless.

  • Bracts stacked like a paper garland, each holding nectar at a depth only one hummingbird's bill can reach — a private fuel line in a crowded market.

  • A metre of blade to catch scraps of light, tapering to a point that drains the surface in minutes so nothing can grow on it.

04 Forest floor · the engine room

Nothing is allowed to pile up

0 – 5 metres

Less than two per cent of the light gets this far, so the floor is surprisingly open — dark, warm and easy to walk through. The jungle of the imagination is really a riverbank or a clearing; under an intact canopy you can see a long way.

Nothing accumulates here. A fallen leaf is gone in six weeks, a whole tree in a few years, because the recycling crew never stops in the heat and wet. Leafcutter ants alone strip up to a sixth of the forest's new growth — not to eat it, but to compost it underground for the fungus they farm. The soil that results is thin and poor: almost the entire nutrient stock of the forest is locked up in the living wood overhead.

The night shift

  • Colonies of eight million running nests eight metres deep, with ventilation shafts and refuse chambers. They are farmers: the leaves feed a fungus, and the fungus feeds them.

  • The forest's largest gardener. It swallows fruit whole and deposits the seeds kilometres away, already packed in fertiliser — big-seeded trees depend on it entirely.

  • One of the few organisms that can digest lignin. Without it, the forest would bury itself in its own fallen timber within a century.

  • Built for scraps: broad, thin, near-black fronds that run photosynthesis on the flecks of light that slip through for a few minutes a day.

“The forest does not grow on the soil. The soil is what is left over when the forest is finished with it.”Below zero · the root zone

Under the litter

The fine roots stay in the top thirty centimetres, sheathed in fungal threads that trade phosphorus for sugar and carry chemical warnings between neighbouring trees. Buttress roots flare sideways rather than dig, holding sixty metres of trunk on ground barely deeper than a bathtub. Cut the canopy and the whole arrangement collapses within a few seasons: the nutrients wash away, and what remains will not grow a forest back.

Four floors, sixty-seven metres, one continuous machine for moving light downwards and carbon upwards. You have just fallen through all of it.
