Notes on the movement

The air

Bagatelle in A minor

Beethoven never published it. The manuscript surfaced forty years after his death in the papers of a Munich schoolmistress, dedicated in a hand so poor that Therese may have been misread as Elise — a scribal accident that gave the world its most familiar tune.

Our pinner took only the first phrase: the trembling semitone, the falling arpeggio, the answer in C. Forty-eight sixteenths, which is exactly one turn of the cylinder and rather less than eight seconds of company.

How it sings

Cylinder, pin & tooth

A brass cylinder is charged with steel pins, each set to the thousandth of an inch. As the spring unwinds, the great wheel turns the cylinder and every pin sweeps down the visible face until it catches the tip of its tooth, bends it, and lets go.

The comb is one piece of tempered steel, slotted into twelve tongues. Long tongues sound low and carry lead weights at the tip; short ones ring high and need none. The tooth does the singing — the case only agrees with it.

At the far end, a fly governor spins in the air to keep the tempo honest. Let the spring run down and you will hear it fail: the air sags, flattens in time, and stops mid-phrase.

Winding

Care of the movement

Turn the crank clockwise until it resists — never past it. A ratchet holds each quarter turn, so you may stop and begin again without losing your work. Eleven full turns fill the barrel.

Wind before playing, not during, and never leave the movement fully wound for years at a time; a spring kept tight forgets how to push. Oil the pivots once a decade with a single drop of clock oil, and keep the lid down against dust — dust in the comb is a silent tooth.

If a tooth buzzes, it is not broken. Something small has lodged between the tongues, and a horsehair will usually persuade it out.
