A Manifesto for Slow Software

Twelve theses in favour of programs that wait for you, an epilogue, and a reading list. Hover any thesis and the page inverts; that is the only trick in the building.

  1. Thesis one

    Attention is a commons, and we have been overgrazing it.

    Every program that opens on your machine draws from the same pasture: the few waking hours in which a person can hold one thing in mind. There is no second field. When a calendar, a chat client and a shopping app each take a bite, they are not competing with each other so much as with the possibility of your having a thought at all.

    Slow software begins by admitting that it is a guest on this land. It takes what it needs to do its job and then it goes quiet.

    Objection
    People can look away whenever they like.
    Reply
    They can, in the way a fish can leave the water. Software that is designed to be hard to look away from has already priced in your willpower.
  2. Thesis two

    Speed is not the same as haste.

    We are not against fast computers. A search that returns in forty milliseconds respects you; a search that returns in forty milliseconds and then keeps suggesting things you did not ask for does not. The distinction is between doing the work quickly and rushing the person. Slow software is fast at the task and slow at the interruption.

    A rule of thumb: measure the time between the user's intent and the user's rest, not the time between the click and the paint.

    Objection
    Users demand instant everything.
    Reply
    They demand that the thing they asked for arrives promptly. Nobody has ever demanded that the thing they did not ask for arrive at all.
  3. Thesis three

    The feed must end.

    A page has a bottom. A newspaper has a back cover. A conversation has a goodbye. The infinite scroll removed the one signal every earlier medium offered for free: the moment when you have finished. Restore it. Paginate. Number the pages. Put a rule at the end and write that is all for today beneath it.

    Objection
    Ending the feed lowers time on site.
    Reply
    Yes. That is the point. Time on site is a cost borne by the visitor and booked as revenue by the host.
  4. Thesis four

    A notification is a tax on the future.

    Each buzz is a small loan taken against the next hour, repaid with interest by the person who has to find their place again. Software that sends ten notifications a day is not helpful ten times; it is expensive ten times. Batch them. Deliver them at hours the reader chose. Make the default none and let people opt into the noise.

    Objection
    Some notifications are genuinely urgent.
    Reply
    A handful a month are. Make those the ones that get through, and the reader will trust the sound again.
  5. Thesis five

    Latency can be honest.

    A spinner that lies about progress is worse than a plain sentence that says this will take about a minute; go and make tea. Slow software tells the truth about time. It does not pretend that a long job is short, and it never fabricates busyness (skeleton screens that shimmer over nothing, progress bars that crawl to ninety-nine and stop) to keep you staring.

    Objection
    Perceived performance matters more than real performance.
    Reply
    Perception is a lever that should be pulled to inform, not to sedate. A truthful estimate is also a perceived-performance technique.
  6. Thesis six

    Defaults are moral choices in disguise.

    Autoplay on. Read receipts on. Email digests daily. "Suggested for you" above "From people you follow". None of these were laws of nature; each was a decision, made in a meeting, that most users will never revisit. The person who sets the default sets the culture. Set it for the quietest reasonable life and let the restless turn things up.

    Objection
    Users can change the settings.
    Reply
    Fewer than one in twenty ever do. The default is the product for the other nineteen.
  7. Thesis seven

    Metrics should be boring.

    If the chart on the wall says daily active users, the software will be built to make people come back daily whether or not they had a reason. Replace it with something dull and humane: tasks completed, minutes saved, sessions ended on purpose. A good tool is measured by how little of the day it needs.

    Ask of every metric: would we still want this number to go up if it made our own mothers miserable?

    Objection
    You cannot manage what you cannot measure.
    Reply
    Agreed. Measure the right thing. Minutes returned to the user is a number, too.
  8. Thesis eight

    Finishing is a feature.

    Version 1.0 was once a milestone; now it is a rumour. Software that changes every fortnight cannot be learned, and what cannot be learned cannot become a habit of the hands. Slow software aims to be done: to reach a shape that is worth keeping, then to protect that shape from its own developers. A tool you can stop thinking about is a tool that has succeeded.

    Objection
    Software that stops changing dies.
    Reply
    Plain text has not changed since 1963 and is doing fine. Stability is what allows a thing to be relied upon.
  9. Thesis nine

    Text first, and for a long time, text only.

    Words load instantly, scale to any screen, survive twenty years, can be searched, copied, quoted, read aloud and translated. Video, autoplaying carousels and animated illustrations are heavier in every sense and they demand the eye. Where a sentence will do, use a sentence. This page has no images and is not the poorer for it.

    Objection
    Video is what people engage with.
    Reply
    People engage with a fire alarm, too. The question is what they wanted, not what held them.
  10. Thesis ten

    Silence is an interface.

    The absence of a badge, the absence of a red dot, the absence of a "what's new" modal: these are design decisions, and they are the hardest ones to defend in a review because they do not demo well. Make them anyway. A screen that does nothing when nothing needs doing is not empty. It is finished.

    Objection
    An empty screen looks broken.
    Reply
    Only to the people who built it. To the person using it, an empty inbox is the best possible news.
  11. Thesis eleven

    The user must own the off switch.

    Quit should mean quit. Delete should mean delete. Unsubscribe should take one click and no guilt. Any program that makes leaving harder than arriving has confessed what it thinks of you. Slow software holds the door: it exports your data in a plain format, it works offline, and it is still there, unchanged, when you come back a year later.

    Objection
    Retention is the lifeblood of a business.
    Reply
    Then earn it. A door that is easy to leave through is the only honest proof that people stayed because they wanted to.
  12. Thesis twelve

    Slow is a discipline, not a speed.

    It is easy to make something noisy; it takes restraint to make something calm. The slow programmer says no to the seventh feature, to the engagement loop, to the analytics package that phones home. Slowness is not a lack of ambition. It is ambition aimed at the right thing: a tool that leaves the person who used it with more of their day than they had before.

    Everything above can be summarised in one line: build software you would be happy to have ignore you.

    Objection
    This is unrealistic in a competitive market.
    Reply
    Every point above has been shipped by someone, somewhere, and paid for by people who were glad to. The market has room for restraint; it simply has not been tried at scale.

A table of speeds

Human rhythms against which any interface should be judged. Anything faster than the leftmost column is the machine's business, not yours.

Approximate durations, for orientation
DurationEventWhat software should do about it
100 msThe threshold below which a response feels instantaneousBe here for typing, clicking and scrolling. Nothing else.
1 sA breath; the limit of an unbroken train of thoughtSay what you are doing in words if you will take longer.
10 sThe point at which a person looks awayLet them. Finish quietly and do not chime.
20 minThe time it takes to recover concentration after an interruptionNever interrupt on your own initiative.
90 minOne cycle of sustained attentionSupport saving, resuming and stopping without penalty.
1 dayThe natural period for a digest of anythingBatch. Deliver once, at a chosen hour.
10 yearsThe reasonable life of a tool worth learningKeep file formats plain and behaviour stable.

Epilogue

This page is built the way it argues. It is one HTML file. It uses the typeface your browser already has, the link colours your browser already knows, and no colour that was not in the original specification. It has no images to download, no script that runs before you ask it to, and it ends. If you scrolled here, you have read the whole thing; there is nothing below the reading list.

You may notice that the one interaction it offers, inverting when you rest on a thesis, is loud. That is deliberate. A single large gesture, chosen once, is quieter over a day than a hundred small ones chosen for you.

Written at a desk, in a text editor, without notifications.
