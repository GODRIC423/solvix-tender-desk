On Lindenmayer systems, and why a grammar can look like a tree

In 1968 the Hungarian-born biologist Aristid Lindenmayer, working in Utrecht, wanted a way to describe how filamentous algae develop — cell by cell, all at once.

He borrowed the idea of a formal grammar and made one decisive change. Where a Chomsky grammar rewrites one symbol at a time, an L-system rewrites every symbol in the string simultaneously, in parallel. Biology forced the choice: in a growing organism every cell divides on its own schedule, and nobody waits their turn.

The smallest garden: algae

An L-system is three things — an alphabet, a starting string called the axiom, and a set of production rules that say what each letter becomes. Lindenmayer's first model of the alga Anabaena catenula used two cell types and two rules: A → AB and B → A. Start from a single A and apply the rules to every letter at once:

  1. n=0A1 cell
  2. n=1AB2
  3. n=2ABA3
  4. n=3ABAAB5
  5. n=4ABAABABA8
  6. n=5ABAABABAABAAB13

The lengths run 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 — Fibonacci numbers. That is no coincidence: A → AB is the Fibonacci recurrence itself, written as a growth process rather than an equation. A mature cell (A) makes a young one (B) and stays; the young one matures. Rabbits, sunflowers, algae: the same rule.

Reading the string with a turtle

The turtle alphabet

F step forward, drawing a line.
f step forward without drawing.

+ and − turn by the angle δ.

[ remember where the turtle is and which way it faces; ] go back there. Any other letter is silent — a placeholder that shapes the grammar without leaving a mark.

A string is only a drawing once something interprets it. Przemysław Prusinkiewicz and Lindenmayer, in The Algorithmic Beauty of Plants (1990), used a turtle: a pen that walks forward on F and rotates on + and −. Without brackets this produces curves — the Koch snowflake, the dragon, the Hilbert curve. The brackets are what turn a curve into a tree. Each [ opens a branch that will return to its parent when the matching ] closes it, so the whole plant can be spoken in one breath, left to right, with no separate notion of a tree at all.

Why they look alive

Two things make these drawings read as botany rather than geometry. The first is self-similarity: a branch is a smaller copy of the whole, because the rule that made the trunk also made every twig. Real plants are only roughly self-similar, but our eye forgives the roughness and recognises the pattern. The second is apical growth: plants extend at their tips, everywhere at once, and an L-system's parallel rewriting reproduces exactly that. Change δ by a few degrees and the species changes — 22.5° gives an oak, 20° a fern, 25.7° a hedgerow shrub — which is also how it works in the field, where a single growth-regulating gene can turn a spreading crown into a spire.

What this garden actually does

Each tree in the canvas above is expanded from its axiom up to a budget of about twenty thousand symbols, then compiled into a flat instruction list. Alongside every F the compiler records its bracket depth and its distance from the root along the path. Depth drives the taper — deeper branches are drawn thinner — and how far each segment bends in the wind, since thin wood swings more than thick. Distance from the root drives the growth animation: on each frame only segments below a rising threshold are drawn, so every tip extends together, the way a real crown fills out, instead of the string being traced from left to right.

Wind is a small, slowly oscillating angle added at every step and scaled by depth, so it accumulates along a branch into a lean that recovers when the gust passes. The season slider interpolates leaf colour from the pale greens of spring through summer into rust and ochre, then thins the canopy by a deterministic hash — the same leaves fall first every year — and drops them as particles that drift downwind before winter leaves only the bare grammar behind.
