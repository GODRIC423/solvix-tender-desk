Grandmother's Bread

A rustic country loaf with a crackling crust and a soft, honeyed crumb. Rosa Benedetti's card, copied out by hand three times since 1962 and still splattered in the same places.

How she did it

  1. 1.Wake the yeast10 min

    Stir the honey into the warm water, sprinkle the yeast over the top and leave it alone. When it looks like the froth on a cappuccino, it is awake and hungry.

  2. 2.Make a well5 min

    Both flours and the salt into the big cream bowl. Push a well into the middle with your fist and pour the yeasty water in. Bring it together with a wooden spoon until there is no dry flour hiding at the bottom.

  3. 3.Knead like you mean it10–12 min

    Tip onto the table. Push away with the heel of your hand, fold back, turn a quarter. Do not add more flour; sticky becomes silky if you keep going. It is ready when it springs back from a poke and the window of stretched dough lets the light through.

  4. 4.First rise1½ hrs

    Back into the bowl, a damp tea towel over the top, somewhere warm but not hot; the airing cupboard, or the chair nearest the stove. Doubled in size is the rule.

  5. 5.Knock back & shape5 min

    Punch the air out, gently. Fold the edges into the centre a few times, then flip and cup your hands round it, turning until it is a tight, proud round. Set it on a floured board, seam down.

  6. 6.Second rise45 min

    Cover again. Meanwhile, heat the oven as hot as it goes, 230°C, with the heavy iron pot inside. Put the kettle on and sit down; this is the only part of the recipe that is for you.

  7. 7.Score and bake40 min

    Dust the top with flour, slash a cross with the sharpest knife you own, and lower it into the hot pot. Lid on for 25 minutes, lid off for 15, until the crust is the colour of a conker.

  8. 8.Wait. Then don't.1 hr, allegedly

    Cool on a rack for a full hour so the crumb sets. Nonna's rule; nobody in this family has ever obeyed it. Tear the end off while it is still too hot and put far too much butter on it.

0 / 8 Nothing ticked yet. Wash your hands, tie your hair back.

Who taught it

Rosa Benedetti learned this loaf from her own mother in a farmhouse outside Lucca, where the oven was a brick mouth in the garden wall and the flour came in sacks you could hide a child behind. She brought the recipe to a terraced house in Bedford in 1962 with two suitcases and no English, and baked it every Saturday for fifty-one years without once weighing anything.

My mother wrote the card you are reading, standing at Rosa's elbow with a set of kitchen scales, translating handfuls into grams and "until it feels right" into minutes. Rosa found the scales hilarious. She would let Mum weigh the flour, then tip a little more in when she thought no one was looking.

I copied it again when the original went soft at the folds. The pencil notes in the margins are Rosa's, the ink ones are Mum's, and the servings slider is mine, because our kitchen is small and our table is not. The bread has never once come out the same twice. That, Rosa said, is how you know a person made it.

— with love, from the drawer by the stove
