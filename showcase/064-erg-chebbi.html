First movement · Arrival الوصول

Erg Chebbiليلة واحدة بين الرمال

One night among the tallest dunes in Morocco, told in four movements. Scroll, and the sky will keep time with you — the sun goes down, the stars come out, the sand turns pink again.

The tarmac gives up at Merzouga. For an hour the road from Rissani has been running dead straight across a black hamada of broken stone, and then, without ceremony, a wall of sand stands up out of the plain: twenty-two kilometres of it, apricot-coloured, a hundred and fifty metres high at the crest. This is the erg. Everything east of it is Algeria.

At four in the afternoon the light is flat and white and the air above the far ridges swims. The dunes do not hold still; their edges bend and pour like glass left too near a flame. Hassan loads the camels — Jimi, Bob, and a young one who has not yet earned a name — and we walk out into it barefoot, because the sand is only just bearable and nobody wants to ride yet.

II

Second movement · Sunset الغروب

The sand turns rose, then violet

We climb the big dune above the camp for the last light, and it is a real climb: two steps up, one sliding back, the ridge as sharp as a folded sheet of paper. From the crest the erg is a sea frozen mid-swell. Every hollow is already filling with blue shadow while every windward face still burns ochre.

The sun swells as it drops and goes the colour of a copper pot. Nobody talks. Hassan points east, where the last ridge fades into a line of violet haze, and says that is where the wind changes direction at night, and that the dune we are standing on will be a hand's width to the west by spring. The sand under my palm cools while I watch.

III

Third movement · Stars النجوم

A sky with no edges

The camp is six tents in a hollow, out of the wind. There is tea with far too much sugar, a tagine that has been sitting in embers since noon, then drums for an hour, and then, quite suddenly, nothing at all. No generator. No road. The nearest streetlight is in Merzouga, behind a hundred metres of sand.

Lie back on the dune and the Milky Way is not a band but a bruise of light from one horizon to the other, thick enough to cast a faint shadow of your own hand. The temperature has fallen twenty-seven degrees since we arrived. Two hand-widths down, the sand is still warm from the afternoon, and that is where you sleep: dug in, blanketed, with the whole sky turning slowly over you.

IV

Fourth movement · Dawn الفجر

First light on every grain

Hassan wakes us in the dark with a hand on the tent pole. The east ridge is a fifteen-minute climb and we make it with breath smoking, in every layer we own. Then the horizon splits: a single line of pink, then a wash of it, then the whole sky above Algeria the colour of the inside of a shell.

The sun comes up fast in the desert. For a few minutes the dunes are cut in two — knife-edge shadow on the west face, sand lit grain by grain on the east — and the ridges throw shadows a kilometre long across the plain. Below us the camels stand up in their odd, back-first way. By the time we reach them the sand is warm again, and the far ridges have begun, very faintly, to swim.
