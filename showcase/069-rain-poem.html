The poem, lying flat

first one drop, then the argument of many

the sky loosens whatever it was holding

each word a small weight let go

gutters learn the grammar of falling

slate roofs, the tin of it, the hush of it

the street becomes a mirror that will not hold still

and the names of things go soft at the edges

everything that falls learns to lie flat,

to widen, to join, to be a surface

where the sky can look at itself

until the light comes back

and takes it home

A note from the author

A concrete poem means with its shape as much as with its words. Rain seemed to ask for one. Rain is already a kind of typography: a page being written on from above, the same few marks repeated until they stop being marks and become a surface.

So the words here do what rain does. Each is let go from the top of the window with a little weight of its own; the heavier, larger words reach the ground sooner, the light ones drift and take their time. They land where they land, on the floor or on whichever word got there first, and settle. Nothing is placed by hand. The pile you see is the poem in the order it was written, only fallen, and no two readers will ever see the same pile.

Once a word has pooled it belongs to the water. Pass your hand over it and it ripples, letter by letter, and the ripple carries to its neighbours, fainter each time, the way a single drop spends itself across a puddle. Every new arrival stirs the pool a little too. When the pool grows too deep it drains: the oldest words sink out through the bottom of the page so that the rain can go on for as long as you care to watch it.

The type is Crimson Pro throughout, a face with enough weight in its serifs to stay legible while falling. The colours are three: the grey-blue of a slate roof in rain, the mist it stands in, and ink. Nothing else seemed necessary, so nothing else was used.

If motion is turned off in your system settings, the words arrive already fallen. The poem is still there. It is only lying down.
