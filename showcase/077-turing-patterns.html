How the pattern grows

Two chemicals, one sheet, no plan.

In 1952 Alan Turing proposed that the spots of a leopard and the stripes of a zebra could arise from nothing more than two substances diffusing through tissue at different speeds while reacting with each other. Nobody had to draw the pattern. The chemistry argues its way into it. Sixty years later biologists found exactly this mechanism ruling the ridges on a mouse's palate and the spacing of shark denticles.

The model on this page

What you are watching is the Gray-Scott system, the most-studied Turing pattern. A chemical u is fed into the dish at a constant rate f. Where it meets two molecules of a second chemical v, it converts into more v. Meanwhile v is removed at rate f + k. Both spread by diffusion, but u spreads twice as fast as v.

∂u/∂t = Du ∇²u − uv² + f (1 − u)
∂v/∂t = Dv ∇²v + uv² − (f + k) v

The whole zoo of behaviour lives in the tiny window of f and k exposed on the sliders. Nudge k by two thousandths and a coral that was branching forever will collapse into a field of self-replicating spots. The coral colour you see is v; the teal rim is the thin boundary where v is being born; cream is the undisturbed sea of u.

How the shader computes it

There is no library here, just raw WebGL. The dish is a floating-point texture with u in the red channel and v in green. Every frame the page runs the update shader several times in a ping-pong: it reads texture A, writes the next state into framebuffer B, then swaps and reads B while writing A. The Laplacian ∇² is a nine-tap stencil, weighting the four neighbours by 0.2 and the diagonals by 0.05, which keeps the isotropy that a simple cross stencil would break.

Your pointer is a uniform. Inside the same update pass, any texel within the brush radius has its v raised, so painting is free: it costs no extra draw call. If the GPU refuses floating-point render targets, the page silently falls back to packing each channel into two bytes for sixteen bits of precision. A final pass reads the state, bilinearly smooths it by hand, and shades the surface with a fake light computed from the gradient of v so the colonies look embossed.
