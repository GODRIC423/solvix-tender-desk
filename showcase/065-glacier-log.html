Sixty-nine days on a saddle of ice that has been stacking winters since before Rome. Six entries follow, one per milestone: the first shallow firn core, the ash layer that dated the borehole, the temperature profile that should not have looked like that, and a melt season that arrived three weeks before the almanac said it would.

  1. 14 Apr 2026Entry IShallow core · 0–12 m

    Camp raised on the divide; the first twelve metres come up like sugar

    Wind on the saddle held under 6 m/s for the whole of the ski-in, which never happens. We set the drill tent by 14:00 and pulled a hand-auger firn core to twelve metres before the light went flat. The upper two metres are last winter, soft and unbonded; you can push a thumb into it. Below that the layers tighten quickly. At 4.2 m there is a thin, glassy crust that I would bet is the warm spell of last August.

    Density climbs the way the textbooks say it should, but the shoulder of the curve sits shallower than in the 1994 pit at the same spot. Warmer summers close the pores earlier.

    • Surface density380kg/m³
    • Density at 12 m562kg/m³
    • Melt crusts / 12 m7
  2. 22 Apr 2026Entry IIDeep drilling · tephra at 41.6 m

    A dark line at 41.6 metres gives the whole borehole a calendar

    Run 19 of the electromechanical drill brought up a 2.4 mm band of grey ash, sharp on both faces, sitting in otherwise clean bubbly ice. Under the hand lens the shards are blocky and unweathered. It has the look and the depth of the 2011 Grímsvötn fallout, which reached this archipelago in a single week of easterlies. If that holds, we have laid down a fixed year in the core.

    Counting annual layers upward from the ash by the summer melt features gives fifteen years, which lands exactly on 2026. The match is almost too clean. I have written it down before I can talk myself out of it.

    • Tephra depth41.6m
    • Layers to surface15
    • Mean accumulation0.74m w.e./yr
  3. 3 May 2026Entry IIIBorehole thermistor string · 0–60 m

    The ten-metre temperature has climbed 1.7 degrees since the last string was lowered

    Left the thermistor string down the shallow hole for forty hours to let the drilling heat bleed out, then read it at dawn. The 10 m value, which is the closest thing a glacier has to a mean annual air temperature, sits at −11.4 °C. Lennart's 1994 profile from the same site reads −13.1 °C. Nothing about the instruments explains a gap that size.

    The curve also bends the wrong way between 20 and 35 m: a warm bulge that means latent heat from refreezing meltwater is being carried down into the firn. The glacier is not just warming from the top. It is being warmed from inside.

    • T at 10 m−11.4°C
    • Change since 1994+1.7°C
    • Warm bulge20–35m
  4. 19 May 2026Entry IVAblation stakes · surface

    Melt onset, three weeks early; the stakes are already losing height

    Positive degree-days began on the 15th. By this morning the snow surface has the grey, granular look of a spring that has made up its mind. The eleven ablation stakes along the transect show a mean surface lowering of 6.1 cm over four days; at the current density that is 4.2 cm of water equivalent per day, roughly twice the long-term May rate.

    Two slush ponds have opened on the lee side of the saddle, each about the size of a tennis court. They are a deep, unreal cyan, and they are exactly where we did not want them: above the deep hole.

    • Ablation rate4.2cm w.e./day

  5. 6 Jun 2026Entry VBedrock contact · 212.3 m

    Bedrock at 212.3 metres: the last core comes up with gravel frozen into its base

    The drill slowed at 211.8 m and came up with a scarred cutter head and a final section that is dark, silty ice with angular gravel, some grains as large as a fingernail. We have touched the bed. The basal metre is bubble-free and bent into folds you can see with the naked eye, which is what you expect from ice that has been dragged across rock for a very long time.

    Field δ¹⁸O from the portable spectrometer runs about −14.8 ‰ in the upper core and drops to −18.1 ‰ near the base: colder precipitation, likely the tail of the Little Ice Age, then something older still. A rough flow-model age for the basal ice is 2 300 years, to be refined at the lab.

  6. 21 Jun 2026Entry VISeason close · 71 core boxes

    Midsummer. The camp is struck and the core leaves the ice before the ice leaves the core

    Seventy-one insulated boxes went down the glacier on two sledges this morning, all logged at −20 °C or colder. The freezer container at the fjord is holding. The section around 115 m, where the bubbles begin to dissolve into the crystal lattice, is the one I will be thinking about on the boat: it is the transition every lab wants and every field team dreads breaking.

    Season mass balance from the stake network comes to −1.38 m water equivalent, the most negative in the record we have for this divide. The saddle lost more in nine weeks than it gained in the previous two winters. The ice remembers everything. That is the point of it, and lately it is also the problem.

    • Bubble–clathrate zone≈115m
