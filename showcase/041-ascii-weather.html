Why a weather station in text

      
Kestrel Ridge sits on a shoulder of rock above the tree line, and for eleven years its instruments have reported over a 1 200 baud packet link that was never meant to carry pictures. So the station learned to draw with what it had: ninety-five printable characters, a fixed-width grid, and one colour of phosphor. Everything on this page keeps to that discipline. There are no images, no icons, no curves that are not made of letters.

      
The sky panel above is a real simulation, not a recording. Rain is a field of particles pulled down by gravity and pushed sideways by a wind value that drifts between frames; each drop is a single glyph chosen by its slope, and a splash is drawn where it meets the ridge. Snow falls slower and wanders on a sine curve, and it accumulates: watch the ground line thicken from ░ to ▒ to ▓ as the drifts build. Sun mode swaps precipitation for a breathing corona and a scatter of heat shimmer. Storm mode raises the wind, doubles the particle count and, every few seconds, traces a lightning path from cloud to ground and inverts the whole panel for a single frame.

      
The inset labelled RADAR is the station's oldest joke. It is a torus rendered by the classic z-buffer method: for every point on the ring we rotate it around two axes, project it with a little perspective, keep only the nearest surface per cell, and pick a character from the ramp .,-~:;=!*#$@ according to how squarely that surface faces the light. It spins whether or not there is any weather to find.

      
The temperature strip beneath the readouts uses the eight Unicode block elements to reach one-eighth-row resolution without leaving the character grid. Hover or arrow across it and the readout follows. None of this needed a framework, a font stack, or a single image request. It needs a monospace face and a little patience.
