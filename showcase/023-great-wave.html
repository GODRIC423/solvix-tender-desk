葛飾北斎 · Katsushika Hokusai · c. 1831

The Great WaveUnder the wave off Kanagawa — an appreciation

One sheet of cherry wood, a handful of Berlin blue, and a mountain hidden in the trough: how a print pulled perhaps eight thousand times became the most reproduced image on earth.

構図
Plate I · The geometry of the break

Hokusai does not paint water. He draws the argument water is making.

Everything in the sheet is bent toward one instant — the half-second in which a swell stops being a hill and becomes a hand. Touch a marker on the print to read what each part of the composition is doing.

波頭

The claw crest

The lip of the wave is not drawn as spray but as fingers — nested crescents that repeat the profile of the whole wave at three smaller sizes. The eye reads the shape twice: once as water, once as a hand about to close. Hokusai had spent forty years drawing hands.

ベロ藍
Plate II · The blue that came by sea

Berlin blue reached Edo before the ships that carried it were welcome.

Japanese printers called it bero-ai — a mangling of “Berlin” — and paid for it in silver until China learned to make it cheaply.

The pigment was stumbled on in a Berlin laboratory around 1706, when a colourmaker's potash turned out to be contaminated with animal oil and produced, instead of a red lake, a blue of terrifying depth. It travelled to Japan first as a curiosity in Dutch and Chinese cargo, priced beyond any print budget. Only at the end of the 1820s, when Chinese workshops flooded the market with a cheaper synthesis, could a publisher put it on a sheet meant to sell for the price of a bowl of noodles.

The timing made the series. Hokusai's publisher, Nishimuraya Yohachi, advertised the Thirty-six Views as printed in the newly imported blue, and the novelty did the selling. It mattered because the older blue — aigami, squeezed from dayflower petals — was fugitive: hang a print in a bright room and its sky drained to grey within a season. Berlin blue held. Two centuries on, the wave in a good impression is still the colour it was the day the paper came off the block.

Bokashi — the graded wash you see above — has no equivalent in the block. The printer wiped pigment across the pear-wood surface with a damp cloth, thinning it toward one edge by hand, and every sheet came out slightly different. It is the one part of a woodblock print that cannot be repeated exactly, which is why collectors argue about impressions rather than about the design.

Plate III · The series it belongs to

The wave is sheet one of forty-six. Fuji is the only thing that never changes.

Hokusai was past seventy when he began Fugaku Sanjūrokkei. The set sold so well that ten further views were cut and the title kept its arithmetic anyway. Drag the row sideways.

No. 33

凱風快晴

Fine Wind, Clear Morning

“Red Fuji.” The mountain fills the sheet at dawn in early autumn, when the south wind turns its flank the colour of a fired pot.

No. 34

山下白雨

Rainstorm Beneath the Summit

The companion to Red Fuji: same silhouette, but lightning forks across the black foot while the peak stands in clear air.

No. 21

神奈川沖浪裏

Under the Wave off Kanagawa

The sheet on this page. Note the title: not “great wave” but under it — the viewer is already in the water.

No. 8

田子の浦

Tago Bay near Ejiri, Tōkaidō

Salt boats in flat water. Hokusai lets the horizon sit unnaturally high so the bay reads as a shelf holding the mountain up.

No. 27

甲州石班沢

Kajikazawa in Kai Province

A fisherman braced on a rock, his lines drawn as a single continuous curve that rhymes with the far slope of Fuji.

No. 30

尾州不二見原

Fujimigahara in Owari

A cooper planes a barrel; the mountain appears inside the hoop. The whole joke of the series in one sheet.

No. 41

深川万年橋下

Under Mannen Bridge at Fukagawa

Fuji framed by the underside of a timber arch — Hokusai's favourite trick: look through something ordinary to find the mountain.

No. 1

江戸日本橋

Nihonbashi in Edo

The series opens in the crowd: storehouse roofs, a packed bridge, and Fuji the size of a thumbnail between them.

摺り
Plate IV · Four hands, one sheet

No woodblock print has an author. It has a workshop.

Hokusai drew; he did not cut and he did not print. The sheet you know is the product of at least four trades, and the publisher's name sat on it as prominently as the artist's.

版下絵

The drawing

Hokusai's brush drawing on thin gampi paper. It was pasted face-down onto cherry wood and cut through — the original was destroyed in the act of reproducing it.

主版

The key block

The carver, horishi, cut away everything that was not line, leaving the black skeleton of the image standing in relief. A week's work for a sheet like this one.

見当

Registration

Two notches — an L and a bar — cut into the corner of every block. The printer laid each sheet against them, which is why eight colours land within a hair's width.

色板

Colour blocks

One block per colour, proofed from the key impression. The wave alone needs three: a pale field, the mid blue, and the near-black of the deepest trough.

馬連

The baren

No press. A coiled-bamboo disc in a bamboo-leaf sheath, worked in circles by hand across the damp paper — the pressure decides how much pigment the fibres take.

ぼかし

Bokashi

The graded wipe, applied to the wet block seconds before printing. Impossible to standardise, which is why no two pulls of the Great Wave are truly identical.

北斎

北斎改為一筆 — “From the brush of Iitsu, formerly Hokusai.”

He signed the sheet with a name he had already outgrown, as he did nine times in a working life of seventy years. At eighty-eight he was still complaining that if heaven granted him five more years he might finally learn to draw a living thing. Impressions of the wave survive in the hundreds; the blocks themselves wore flat and were thrown away.
