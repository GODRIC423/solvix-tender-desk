02 · THEORY

Why the wheel works

Five short chapters on what the circle encodes, what it leaves out, and how to turn a harmony into a palette you can actually ship.

Chapter one

Hue is an angle

The visible spectrum is a straight line from red to violet, yet Newton bent it into a ring in 1704 because the two ends look related. Violet leans back toward red through magenta, a colour that appears in no rainbow and exists only as a mixture. Once the line closes into a circle, every hue becomes an angle: red at 0°, yellow at 60°, green at 120°, cyan at 180°, blue at 240°, magenta at 300°.

That single move is what makes harmony computable. Opposite means 180° apart. Neighbouring means about 30° apart. The wheel above stores hue as a number, so a scheme is nothing more mysterious than addition.

Chapter two

Complements cancel, so they vibrate

Two hues 180° apart are complementary. Mixed as light they produce neutral grey; that is literally what the word means, each supplies what the other lacks. Placed side by side, though, they do the opposite of cancelling. Your eye adapts to one hue and the after-image of that adaptation is its complement, so each colour pushes the other toward greater apparent saturation. Chevreul described this simultaneous contrast in 1839, and Itten built a whole teaching method on it.

Chapter three

Analogous is quiet, triadic is loud

Analogous hues sit within roughly 60° of each other. They share an undertone, so a page built on them reads as one temperature and one mood; think of autumn leaves or a sea at dusk. The cost is contrast: an analogous scheme has to earn its emphasis through lightness rather than hue.

Triads spread three hues evenly at 120°. Every pair is far apart, so the scheme is energetic almost by default, which is why it dominates children's brands and sports kits. Split-complementary softens the same idea, replacing the direct opposite with its two neighbours, so you keep the tension and lose the shouting.

Chapter four

Saturation and lightness do the real work

The wheel is honest about hue and vague about everything else. Move the big handle toward the centre and the colour desaturates toward grey; slide the lightness control and the whole scheme lifts to pastel or drops to something inky. In practice a shipped palette leans on those two axes far more than on the angle plan.

A useful discipline is 60 / 30 / 10: one hue for six tenths of the surface, mostly at low saturation; a second for three tenths; the loudest for the last tenth, where you want the eye to land. The grid below is your base hue stepped through saturation and lightness, the material the plan is actually made from.

Chapter five

Warm and cool

Cut the circle from roughly 330° to 150° and you have the warm half: reds, oranges, yellows and the first greens. Warm hues appear to advance and cool hues to recede, a cue painters have used for atmosphere since the Renaissance and interface designers still use to bring a button forward off a calm ground.

Temperature is relative, not absolute. A violet beside a red looks cool; the same violet beside a blue looks warm. When a scheme feels dead, the fix is often not a new hue but a temperature shift inside the hue you already have.
