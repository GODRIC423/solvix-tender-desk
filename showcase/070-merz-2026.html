Apparatus No. 1

The cut-up engine

Below this page sits our manifesto, set in orderly columns like a good citizen. The button takes a pair of scissors to it. Every word is cut loose, dropped into a bag, shaken, and drawn out one at a time — the machine does the shaking, you supply the scissors and the nerve.

“Take a newspaper. Take a pair of scissors. Choose an article the length you intend your poem to be. Cut out the article. Then cut out each of the words and put them in a bag. Shake gently. Take out each scrap one after the other. Copy conscientiously. The poem will resemble you.” — Tristan Tzara, 1920

No poem is stored, repeated or improved. When you leave the page it is gone, which is the only honest way to publish.

What we mean by Merz

Merz is the eighth syllable of the word Kommerz, torn out of a bank advertisement and glued to a picture. That is the entire method. Take a thing made to sell you something, cut away the part that does the selling, keep the syllable that is left over, and call it art. We have been doing this for a hundred and ten years and the banks have still not noticed.

MERZ 2026 is an anti-journal. It has no editorial line, no masthead you should trust, no schedule, and no back issues, because we paste each new one directly over the last. Our contributors this month are a tram conductor, three algorithms that were let go in the spring, a woman who collects the receipts other people drop on the pavement, and Hannah, who refuses to be described.

We are against the smooth rectangle. We are against the grid that arrives before the idea. We are against the interface that anticipates you so completely that you are left with nothing to want. Everything on this page can be picked up and put somewhere worse, and we would consider it a courtesy if you did.

A note on the scissors. Tzara wrote the recipe in 1920 and it still works because it costs nothing and insults everybody. Cut out the article. Cut out each word. Shake the bag gently. Copy conscientiously. The poem will resemble you, which is either the promise or the threat, depending on the poem.

Do not ask what it means. Meaning is a service industry and it is fully staffed. What we offer instead is friction: paper that tears where you did not want it to tear, glue that shows at the edges, type that refuses to line up, a red stamp applied by nobody, and a small honest mess that you made yourself by dragging things around a screen at some hour when you should have been asleep.

Numbered out of order, deliberately

Instructions for making a poem

Order is a rumour started by furniture.

Follow these in any sequence, or invert them, or perform only the ones you can afford. There is no correct number of steps and there never was; we printed the receipt anyway because a receipt makes anything look official, and looking official is the last joke we have left.

Should you complete all of them in the given order, you have misunderstood the exercise and must begin again with a duller pair of scissors, at midnight, in a language you do not speak.

  1. XI. Buy the newspaper you disagree with.
  2. IV. Cut. Do not read while cutting.
  3. XVII. Lose one word on the floor. Leave it there.
  4. II. Shake the bag as if it owed you money.
  5. IX. Copy conscientiously, misspellings included.
  6. VII. Stamp it in red. Sign nothing.
  7. I. Read it aloud to somebody who will not be polite.
  8. XIII. Paste the next one over it tomorrow.
TotalNothing

Colophon, such as it is

Set in Abril Fatface and Cutive Mono, the only two faces still in the drawer. Torn edges generated at load, differently for every visitor, and never twice the same. Sepia, paper, black, and one red stamp: no other colour was purchased.

Built as a single file with no libraries, no trackers, no analytics and no back end — there is nowhere for your poem to go, which we consider a feature. Reproduce freely, badly, and without attribution: back to the pile.
