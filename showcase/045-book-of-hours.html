Incipit liber horarum · Here begins the book

The Book of Hours

A leaf concerning the eight canonical hours, with a six-line initial, and a note on gold leaf, ultramarine and vermilion.

Before the clock, the day was sung. A medieval household did not divide its hours into sixty equal minutes; it divided them into prayers. Eight times between one midnight and the next, the bells of the nearest church gave the hour its name — Matins in the dark, Lauds at first light, and so on to Compline, when the fire was covered and the door barred. The Book of Hours was the layman's and, above all, the laywoman's copy of that rhythm: a portable, private devotion, small enough to be carried to Mass in a sleeve and costly enough to be named in a will.

¶These were the best-selling books of the Middle Ages. Between about 1250 and 1550 more Books of Hours were made than any other kind of book, first by hand and then in print. The finest were painted for dukes and duchesses by masters such as the Limbourg brothers, Jean Pucelle and Simon Marmion; the plainest were stock copies sold from a stall in Paris or Bruges, with blank shields in the borders where a buyer's arms might later be added.

¶The text of every Book of Hours is built around the Little Office of the Blessed Virgin, wrapped in a calendar of feast days, extracts from the Gospels, the Penitential Psalms, a litany of saints and the Office of the Dead. Around the words grew the pictures: a full-page miniature at the head of each hour, borders of ivy, acanthus and strawberry, and the initials — the great painted letters, six lines tall, that open each office and that this leaf is about.

Press the initial to see the scribe's underdrawing beneath the gold. Move your hand across the page and the leaf catches the light.

Horæ canonicæ · the eight hours of the day

Nunc — it is the hour of …

  1. Matinsad noctem · in the small hours

    Domine, labia mea aperies. Lord, open my lips; and my mouth shall declare thy praise. The longest of the hours, opened with the invitatory psalm Venite exsultemus and read by candle before dawn.

    Miniature: the Annunciation

  2. Laudsad auroram · at daybreak

    Named for the three laudate psalms, 148 to 150, sung as the light returns, and closed with Zechariah's canticle, the Benedictus.

    Miniature: the Visitation

  3. Primehora prima · about six

    The first hour, said on rising. Short, and asking a blessing on the day's work: Deus in adiutorium meum intende.

    Miniature: the Nativity

  4. Tercehora tertia · about nine

    The third hour, when the Spirit descended at Pentecost; hence the hymn Veni Creator Spiritus. Work paused for the length of three psalms.

    Miniature: the Annunciation to the Shepherds

  5. Sexthora sexta · midday

    The sixth hour, the sun at its height, the hour of the Crucifixion. Said before the midday meal, and brief for that reason.

    Miniature: the Adoration of the Magi

  6. Nonehora nona · about three

    The ninth hour, at which Christ died. Its name survives in the English noon, which crept earlier through the centuries as the dinner hour did.

    Miniature: the Presentation in the Temple

  7. Vespersad vesperas · at lamp-lighting

    Magnificat anima mea Dominum. The evening office, sung as the lamps are lit; the hour most often heard by the laity in church.

    Miniature: the Flight into Egypt

  8. Complinecompletorium · before sleep

    Nunc dimittis servum tuum, Domine. The day is completed. Simeon's canticle, the Salve Regina, and silence until Matins.

    Miniature: the Coronation of the Virgin

The pointing hand marks the present hour by your own clock. Open an hour to read its antiphon.

De auro et lapide · a note on the materials

Of gold leaf & ultramarine

Why blue and gold together still read as luxury: because, by weight, they were.

Gold

Gold on a manuscript page is not paint. It is metal, beaten from a coin into leaf thinner than a hair — one ducat could be hammered into a hundred leaves the size of a hand. The illuminator first laid a raised bed of gesso: chalk, white lead, a little sugar or honey to keep it supple, and Armenian bole, the red clay that gives old gilding its warm cast wherever the leaf has worn through.

¶He breathed on the gesso to make it tacky, laid the leaf with a gilder's tip, and burnished it with a dog's tooth or a polished agate set in a handle until the hammered surface closed and threw back the light like a mirror. That is why the gold in a Book of Hours still flashes when the page is tilted toward a window: it is being lit, not looked at. The gold on this leaf follows your hand for the same reason. Where hairlines were wanted — the sprays in the borders — powdered gold was mixed with gum and kept in a mussel shell: shell gold, laid with a brush and never burnished.

Vermilion

The red is vermilion, mercury and sulphur heated together until they turn scarlet. It was the rubricator's colour — from rubrica, red earth — reserved for the headings and instructions that told the reader where each hour began. Everything on this leaf in red is a rubric, and everything in black was written before it.

Ultramarine

The blue of the initial is ultramarine, ground from lapis lazuli mined at Sar-i Sang in Badakhshan and carried ultra marinum, beyond the sea, to Venice. Extracting it was slow. Cennino Cennini's Libro dell'arte describes kneading the ground stone into a dough of pine resin, mastic and wax, then working the lump under warm lye: the blue washes out first, purest and dearest, while the grey rock stays behind. Later washings gave paler grades; the last was sold as ultramarine ash.

¶At its best the pigment cost more than the gold beside it, and contracts sometimes fixed by weight how much a painter must use — always on the Virgin's mantle. Cheaper books made do with azurite, which is greener and darkens with age.

Explicit liber horarum.

This leaf was ruled with a dry point, written in oak-gall ink, rubricated in vermilion and illuminated with leaf and lapis. The border grows as the page is opened; the gold answers the light; the hand points to the hour.
