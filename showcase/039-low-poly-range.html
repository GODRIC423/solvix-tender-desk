01 — Note

Why triangles are the whole trick

Everything on the screen above that looks like a mountain is a grid. Forty-odd columns by eighteen rows of vertices, each one nudged sideways a little so the lattice stops looking like graph paper, each one lifted by a value read from a noise field. Two triangles per cell, and the triangles are the point: three points always sit on a single plane, so a triangle has exactly one normal and therefore exactly one colour. A quad would not cooperate — four points almost never share a plane, and a renderer would have to invent a bend across the middle.

The shading is deliberately flat. For every face we take the cross product of two edges to get its normal, dot that against the direction of the sun, and clamp. The result is a single number between zero and one that says how squarely the face looks at the light. That number picks a point on the ramp from dusk purple through coral to peach. No interpolation across the face, no gradients, no blurring between neighbours. The seams between triangles are the picture, not a flaw in it.

Depth is faked twice. Rows further from the camera are squeezed towards the horizon, and their colours are pulled towards the sky by the haze control — the same aerial perspective painters have leaned on since the fifteenth century, reduced to one linear interpolation per face. Snow is a height threshold rather than a texture: any face whose centroid climbs above the snowline is blended towards white, and the blend still respects the light, so the shaded sides of the summits stay a cold lilac while the sunward sides burn.

Because the geometry is rebuilt from a seed, the range is reproducible. The hex value in the corner of the control card is the entire mountain: feed it back in and you get the same ridges, the same diagonals, the same single valley that happened to open up in the middle.

02 — Recipe

How a range is built in six steps

  1. Seed

    A 32-bit integer drives a tiny mulberry32 generator. The same seed always rebuilds the same range, down to the last diagonal.

  2. Noise

    Five octaves of ridged value noise. Each octave is folded — 1 − |2n − 1| — so valleys stay soft and crests come out sharp.

  3. Lattice

    Vertices are jittered up to a third of a cell in both directions. Boundary vertices stay pinned so the mesh still meets the frame.

  4. Split

    Each cell chooses its diagonal at random. Alternating diagonals read as a herringbone; random ones read as rock.

  5. Light

    The sun-angle slider moves a direction vector across the sky. One dot product per face turns geometry into colour; nothing is interpolated.

  6. Haze

    Faces are mixed toward the horizon colour by depth^1.4 × haze, so the far ridge dissolves long before the near one does.
