Explicatio Tabulae

Explanation of the fourth plate, with the numbers answering to the index.

The figure shows the left hand of an adult seen from the back — the dorsum manus — with the forearm cut short a little above the wrist. It was drawn at natural size and reduced by one third for the plate. Three states of the same figure are laid upon a single ground, so that each may be viewed alone or one upon another; where two are shown together the upper is cut in open line, that the lower may be read through it.

In the first state the skin is entire. The nails (1) are drawn with their roots hidden beneath the fold of skin at their base; the folds over the joints (2) mark the places where the phalanges meet and thicken with age and labour; and the veins of the back of the hand (3) are shown as they appear when the arm hangs down and the vessels swell, making the arched net that every draughtsman knows.

In the second state the skin and the fat beneath it are removed. The long tendons of the common extensor of the fingers (5) pass beneath their ligament at the wrist (6), which holds them to the bone as a strap holds a bundle of reeds, and run along the back of each finger, where they spread into a thin sheet. Between the bones of the metacarpus lie the four dorsal interosseous muscles (7), which draw the fingers apart from one another. The tendon of the long extensor of the thumb (8) crosses obliquely to the last joint of that digit, and on the outer border the abductor of the little finger (9) fills the space between the wrist and the knuckle.

In the third state every soft part is taken away and the twenty-seven bones remain: fourteen phalanges (10, 11, 12), five bones of the metacarpus (13), and the eight small bones of the carpus (14), set in two rows and bound to the ends of the radius and ulna (15). The thumb alone wants a middle phalanx, and it is this want, together with the freedom of its first joint, that lets it be set against the tips of the other fingers — the office on which the whole use of the hand depends.

The numbers upon the plate answer to those in the index beside it. Where a hairline crosses a part to which it does not belong, the reader will forgive the engraver: the parts lie close together, and the paper is small.

De modo sculpendi

On the manner of the cutting. The shading is laid in parallel hatchings that follow the round of each part; where the shadow deepens a second set of lines is drawn across the first at about sixty degrees, and where the light falls the paper is left bare. The bones are shaded on the side away from the light, the tendons lengthwise, the ligament of the wrist across.

No colour is used but the sepia of the ink and the tone of the paper, as was the custom of the plates from which this one takes its manner.
