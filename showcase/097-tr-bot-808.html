Operation Manual

Section 3 — Writing a Rhythm

1. The four voices

The bass drum is a single sine oscillator whose pitch is swept from roughly 150 Hz down to 45 Hz in a twelfth of a second; the drop is what your ear reads as weight, not the volume. A short click of filtered noise sits on top so the attack survives on small speakers.

The snare is two things glued together: a pair of detuned triangle tones near 180 Hz for the body, and a burst of high-passed noise for the wires under the head. The hi-hat stacks six square waves at inharmonic ratios and pushes them through a narrow band-pass — that is why it rings like metal rather than hissing like static.

The hand clap is four noise bursts fired eleven milliseconds apart into a resonant filter, then a longer tail. Four people never clap in perfect unison, and neither does the machine.

2. Step writing

Each row holds sixteen steps — one bar of sixteenth notes. Tap a pad once for a normal hit, twice for an accent. Accents are not just louder: the bass drum gets a longer tail, the snare a sharper crack, and the hi-hat opens up instead of closing.

Start with the bass drum on step 1. Put the snare on 5 and 13. Everything else is decoration. If a pattern feels stiff, take something away before you add anything — the space between hits is the instrument.

3. Signal path

Every voice walks the same five stages. Only the source and the filter change from drum to drum — the compressor and the master stage are shared, which is what makes the kit sit together as one instrument instead of four.

4. Shuffle & tone

SHUFFLE delays every off-beat sixteenth by a percentage of the step length. A little under 20% swings; past 45% it limps on purpose, which is occasionally exactly right. TONE opens the decay of the drums and lifts the hat's band-pass together, taking the kit from tight and dry to long and glassy.

Tempo is free from 60 to 200 BPM. The scheduler runs a hundred milliseconds ahead of the sound card, so the timing stays steady even while you are dragging a knob.
