§ I — The Reading

Thirteen full moons, and one of them spare.

Nothing in this almanac was copied from a table. Every disc you see on this page was computed the moment it loaded, from the same periodic terms an ephemeris uses: the moon's mean longitude carried forward by its anomaly, the sun's apparent longitude, and the difference between them — the elongation — turned into an angle of illumination and then into light falling across a sphere.

2026 is a generous year. It opens with a full moon on the third of January, before the decorations are down, and closes with another on Christmas Eve. Between them fall thirteen full moons rather than the usual twelve, because a lunation is eleven days shorter than the calendar year and the surplus has to go somewhere. It goes into May, which receives two: one on the first, one on the thirty-first. The second is a blue moon, a name that has nothing to do with colour and everything to do with bookkeeping.

The moon at the top of this page is not a drawing. It is a sphere of maria and craters lit from a direction derived from tonight's phase angle, with the terminator falling where the geometry says it must, and the unlit limb raised very slightly by earthshine — the sunlight our own planet throws back at it, which is why a young crescent carries a grey ghost of the whole disc inside its horns.

Eclipses of 2026

  • 17 FebAnnular solar. A ring of fire over Antarctica; partial from the southern ocean and the tip of Africa.
  • 3 MarTotal lunar. The Worm Moon passes wholly into shadow — the Pacific, the Americas and East Asia keep watch.
  • 12 AugTotal solar. The great one: totality across Greenland, Iceland and northern Spain in the late evening light.
  • 28 AugPartial lunar. A bite taken out of the Sturgeon Moon, visible from the Americas, Europe and Africa.

Eclipse days are marked in the calendar with an indigo point.

                          § III — On keeping one

                          Keep it by the window.

                          An almanac is a promise made in advance and kept in public. It is checkable: go outside on the twenty-sixth of September, look east around sunset, and the disc that comes up over the roofline will be the disc printed here, or this page was wrong. Very little else on the internet offers that.

                          The habit worth forming is smaller than astronomy. Note the moon once a week — the phase, the hour, where on the horizon it appeared — and by the end of the year you will have learned, without ever meaning to, that the moon rises about fifty minutes later each night, that a crescent never appears at midnight, and that the full moon of December stands where the sun stood in June. These are not facts you memorise. They are facts you notice, and noticing is the whole of the discipline.

                          Method

                          Phase instants after Jean Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms (2nd ed.), ch. 49; daily elongation and illuminated fraction from the abridged lunar and solar series of ch. 45–47. Computed in your browser at load, geocentric, times in UTC, good to a minute or two.

                          The rendering

                          The moons are drawn pixel by pixel on a canvas: a procedural albedo map of maria and craters over a bump field, lit by a Lambert term from the true phase angle, with limb darkening and a trace of earthshine on the night side.

                          Folklore

                          Month names follow the Algonquian and Old English traditions as collected by the farmers' almanacs of the eighteenth century. Where the two disagree, both are given.
