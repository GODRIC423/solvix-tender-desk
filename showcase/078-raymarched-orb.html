Orb

A sphere that exists only as a signed distance function: for any point in space, one number says how far away the surface is. A ray steps by that number, again and again, until the number reaches zero. The colour is a thin oil film, computed per wavelength.

Notes on the shader

Nothing here is a mesh. The whole scene is one function of position.

Signed distance functions

A signed distance function takes a point and returns the shortest distance to a surface, negative on the inside. For a sphere it is embarrassingly short: length(p) − r. The orb above is that sphere with a slow, low-frequency displacement added to the radius so the surface breathes, and the whole thing lives inside a single fragment shader with no geometry uploaded at all. Two triangles cover the screen; everything else is arithmetic.

Because the function is analytic, there is nothing to tessellate. Zoom in forever and the silhouette stays a curve. The normal is not stored either — it is the gradient of the field, estimated with four evaluations arranged as a tetrahedron, which is cheaper than the usual six and just as smooth.

Marching without missing

Rendering is sphere tracing. From the camera, a ray asks the field how far the nearest surface is, then moves forward by exactly that amount. It cannot overshoot, because a sphere of that radius around the ray is guaranteed empty. Near a silhouette the steps shrink and pile up, which is why raymarchers spend most of their budget on edges; the shader stops after ninety-six steps or once the distance drops under a fraction of a millimetre in scene units.

The blackness around the orb is not laziness. Rays that escape past a far bound are painted with a faint vignette and a soft bloom whose colour is borrowed from the light, so the sphere appears to sit in a room rather than in a void.

Colour from thickness, not from paint

The iridescence is a physically motivated thin-film interference model. Imagine an oil layer a few hundred nanometres deep. Light reflecting from its top surface interferes with light reflecting from the bottom; whether a wavelength adds or cancels depends on the optical path 2·n·d·cos θ. The shader evaluates that phase separately for red, green and blue wavelengths, so the same patch of surface turns violet, teal or amber depending on how steeply you look at it.

Film thickness is itself a function of position and time — a slow noise field — so the colour bands drift across the surface like soap on water. The mouse moves the key light; the light drives both the specular lobe and the incidence angle, so pulling it across the screen sweeps the entire spectrum around the orb. A Fresnel term keeps the centre glassy and the rim bright, and a soft white highlight anchors the form.
