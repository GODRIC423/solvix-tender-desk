Family bar

The coloured rule along the top is the flavour family. Five families, five spice-market colours: turmeric, chilli, cumin, mint and pepper. Neighbouring tiles share a bar, so the table reads as regions before it reads as names.

Number & intensity

The catalogue number counts left to right, top to bottom, exactly as atomic numbers do. The five dots beside it rate intensity, from a whisper (one) to something that rearranges the dish (five).

Symbol

Two letters, invented for this chart. Some are obvious (Cu is cumin, Sf is saffron); some borrow the chemist's habit of reaching for the second consonant. Learn the symbols and the families' regions become a map you can navigate blind.

Origin & the flip

The origin line names where the plant was first cultivated or traded, not where it grows today. Hover, tap, or focus a tile and it turns over to show four pairings that reliably work, with the full data sheet in the readout above.

The five families

Fig. 2 · Regions of the chart
Columns 1–211

Herbal & Fresh

The left-hand block, like the alkali metals: reactive, quick to fade, best added late. These are leaves and stalks rather than seeds, and their volatile oils flash off in minutes. Row one holds mint, the gentlest; by row six the resinous heavies, bay and lemongrass, need a long simmer to give anything up.

Columns 3–812

Earthy & Nutty

The transition block in the middle: the seed drawer. Coriander, fennel, caraway, sesame, poppy and celery seed sit above cumin, fenugreek, nigella, mustard, ajwain and asafoetida. Every one of them improves with a dry toast in a hot pan, and every one of them is the difference between a stew and a curry.

Golden & Warm

Bark, pods, buds and threads: the sweet spices of the old trade routes, and the ones a pastry kitchen cannot open without. They are the metalloids of this chart, sitting between the seeds and the fire and borrowing from both. Vanilla and cinnamon begin the region; clove, numbing and medicinal, closes it.

Staircase 11–138

Fiery

One genus, Capsicum, arranged as a diagonal staircase because heat is a gradient, not a switch. Paprika and Aleppo barely register; ancho and gochugaru bring fruit before fire; chipotle adds smoke; Kashmiri adds colour. Cayenne and habanero anchor the bottom row, where the numbers stop being polite.

Columns 13–147

Pungent & Sharp

The noble column on the far right, home to the true peppers and the rhizomes that behave like them. Black, white and long pepper share a vine; ginger and galangal share a root; Sichuan pepper is neither, a citrus husk that buzzes the tongue. Grains of paradise finish the chart the way pepper finishes a plate.
