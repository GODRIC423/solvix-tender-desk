The Green Hour

Introduction

Absinthe is not a flavour so much as a landscape. It begins in the high, cold meadows of the Val-de-Travers, where grand wormwood grows silver against the limestone, and it ends in a café at five o'clock, when a slotted spoon is laid across a glass and iced water is let down, drop by drop, until the clear green turns to opal. The French called that hour l'heure verte; the drink itself they called la fée verte, the green fairy, and painted her onto posters with whiplash hair and a wormwood crown.

Beneath the myth lies a very disciplined recipe. A true absinthe verte is a maceration and distillation of a small, sacred trinity — wormwood, green anise and fennel — coloured afterwards by a second, gentler steeping of finishing herbs whose chlorophyll lends the peridot hue. Hyssop and melissa are the classic colouring pair. Every one of the five is a plant a nineteenth‑century apothecary would have kept in a labelled jar, and each is drawn here as the poster artists of that era might have drawn it: framed in vine, softened at every corner, and set in a curve rather than a row.

Herbarium

Five Herbs of the Verte

Three in the still, two in the colouring bath. Follow the vine downward from the bitterest to the sweetest.

1

Grand Wormwood

Artemisia absinthium · the bitter heart

The plant that named the drink. Its felted, silver‑grey leaves are among the most bitter things in the herbal, and it is the source of thujone, the compound blamed — mostly wrongly — for a century of moral panic. In the still it gives a dry, sage‑like backbone; without it there is no absinthe, only pastis.

2

Green Anise

Pimpinella anisum · the sweet cloud

A frail Mediterranean umbellifer whose ribbed seeds carry anethole, the oil that makes absinthe taste of liquorice and, more importantly, makes it louche. Anethole dissolves in strong spirit but not in water, so as the glass is diluted it falls out of solution as a fine emulsion — the milky cloud you watch for.

3

Fennel

Foeniculum vulgare · the round shoulder

Where anise is sharp, fennel is soft. Its seed shares anethole but wraps it in a warm, faintly camphorous sweetness that rounds the wormwood's edge and lends body to the louche. Distillers speak of the trinity in proportion: fennel is the herb you add to make the other two agree.

4

Hyssop

Hyssopus officinalis · the colour and the mint

A low, woody shrub of the Alpine foothills, mentioned in scripture as a cleansing herb. Added after distillation, its leaves release the deep chlorophyll that makes a verte verte, along with a cool, camphor‑mint note that lifts the finish. Its blossoms are violet in the field; the poster tradition let us paint them in terracotta.

5

Melissa

Melissa officinalis · lemon balm, the bright thread

Named for the bees that adore it, melissa is the gentlest of the five. Its crinkled, heart‑shaped leaves smell unmistakably of lemon peel and honey, and in the colouring bath they add a citrus lift that keeps the finished spirit from tasting merely of herbs and sugar. It is the last note you taste, and the one that makes you pour again.

Chronicle

From Couvet to the Ban, and Back

Two centuries in eight moments — the rise, the fall and the quiet return of the green fairy.

  1. 1792
    An elixir in the Val-de-Travers

    In Couvet, Switzerland, the Henriod sisters — and, in the more colourful telling, a French doctor named Pierre Ordinaire — sell a wormwood tonic as a cure for everything from worms to melancholy.

  2. 1805
    Pernod Fils crosses the border

    Henri‑Louis Pernod opens a distillery at Pontarlier, just inside France, to avoid Swiss duties. Pontarlier becomes the capital of absinthe, and the Pernod name its shorthand.

  3. 1840s
    The army takes a taste

    French soldiers in Algeria are issued absinthe as a fever preventive and water purifier. They bring the habit home, and a medicine becomes an aperitif.

  4. 1860s
    L'heure verte

    Five o'clock in Paris turns green. Cafés set out fountains and slotted spoons; Manet, Degas, Verlaine and later Toulouse‑Lautrec make the glass a fixture of modern life.

  5. 1905
    The Lanfray affair

    A Swiss labourer murders his family after a day that included two glasses of absinthe — and a great deal of wine and brandy. The temperance press names only the absinthe.

  6. 1915
    The ban

    Switzerland (1910) and the United States (1912) have already outlawed it; wartime France follows. Distillers pivot to anise pastis, and the wormwood fields go quiet.

  7. 1988
    A limit, not a ban

    European rules set a permitted thujone level for spirits, legalising properly made absinthe across most of the continent almost by accident. Small distillers begin reading old recipes.

  8. 2005–07
    Home again

    Switzerland repeals its ban and Couvet distils openly once more; the United States approves the first compliant absinthes in 2007. The green hour resumes, a little wiser.

The Ritual

How to Louche

No fire, no theatre. The traditional preparation is slow, cold and quiet — the entire point is to watch.

  1. Measure the dose

    Pour about 30 ml of absinthe into a reservoir glass, filling the bulb at its base. The reservoir is your measure; you never need a jigger.

  2. Lay the spoon

    Rest a slotted absinthe spoon across the rim and set a single sugar cube on it. Sugar is optional with a well‑made verte, but the slow dissolve is half the pleasure.

  3. Let the water fall

    Drip iced water over the sugar — from a fountain, a carafe, or simply a steady hand — three to five parts water to one part spirit. Never in a rush: the cloud needs time.

  4. Watch the louche, then drink

    Oils of anise and fennel bloom into opal trails, the green pales to jade and the aroma opens. Stir the last of the sugar, sip, and note the order in which the herbs arrive.
