The Keepers

For two centuries, men and women climbed a hundred stairs at dusk to keep a flame alive for strangers they would never meet. This is what the work asked of them, and what it gave back.

I

The Watch

Half an hour before sunset the keeper climbed. The stair was the same stair every night, a stone spiral inside a stone tube, and at the top of it was a room made entirely of glass in which a lamp had to be lit at an exact minute and kept burning until an exact minute the following morning. That was the whole of the job. Everything else was only the work of making it possible.

The lamp had to be trimmed, because a wick that smoked would soot the lens and dull the light by half before midnight. The clockwork that turned the lens had to be wound, a weight the size of a paint tin hauled up the well of the tower every few hours and let fall again, tick by tick, to spin four tons of glass. The storm panes had to be wiped of salt. The oil had to be warmed in winter so it would draw. The log had to be written: wind, sea, visibility, vessels passing, any failure of the light and the reason for it.

Keepers worked in watches, usually of four hours, and the middle watch, one until five, was the one nobody wanted. It was the watch in which the body most wanted to sleep and the light most needed a witness. There was a chair in the lantern room, but by custom and in some services by regulation it was a hard one. A keeper found asleep on watch was dismissed, not for laziness but because a light that goes out at three in the morning is a lie told to every ship that has trusted it.

What strikes you, reading the old logbooks, is how little of the work was heroic and how much of it was maintenance. Brass was polished every day. Lenses were dusted with a badger-hair brush and never touched by a finger. The keeper's real enemy was not the storm but entropy, the slow way that anything left alone at sea returns to rust and salt.

II

Rock, Reef and Shore

Not all lighthouses were equal, and the keepers knew the hierarchy by heart. A shore station stood on the mainland, with a garden, a road and a school within walking distance; a keeper's children grew up there and his wife was very often the unpaid assistant. An island station was harder: supplies by boat, school by correspondence, a doctor by prayer. And then there were the rocks.

A rock station was a tower built on something the tide covered twice a day. Bell Rock, eleven miles off the Angus coast, is under water for all but a few hours of each low tide; Robert Stevenson's men in 1807 worked in a rush between tides and lived on a ship anchored nearby. Once the tower was lit in 1811 there was no land at all, only the tower, a landing stage and the sea. Keepers served a month on and a fortnight off, weather permitting, and the weather frequently did not permit. Reliefs were missed by weeks. Men ran out of tobacco, then sugar, then patience.

The isolation had a specific texture. It was not the silence, keepers said, because a rock is never silent. It was the knowledge that whatever happened, nobody was coming. In 1801 at the Smalls, off Pembrokeshire, one of two keepers died in a storm. His colleague, afraid that he would be accused of murder if he committed the body to the sea, built a coffin from the interior panelling and lashed it to the gallery rail, where it stayed for weeks while the weather held the relief boat off. When the boat finally came, the survivor was found alive but changed. From then on British rock lights carried three men, never two.

In December 1900 the relief tender arrived at the Flannan Isles, west of Lewis, to find the light out, the door shut, the clocks stopped and the three keepers gone. The log ran to the fifteenth. Nothing else was ever found. The story has been told so often that it has been polished into legend, but to keepers it was simply a warning about the one thing the job could not protect them from, which was the sea itself, arriving all at once.

Three kinds of station
  • Shore. Mainland, road access, families in residence. The keeper's wife often served as unofficial assistant.
  • Island. Boat relief, a small community of two or three families, a vegetable plot and a great many sheep.
  • Rock. Tower only. Three men, a month on. Bell Rock, Skerryvore, the Smalls, Eddystone, Fastnet.
III

What the Weather Wanted

Every tower is an argument with the weather, and the weather has won more often than the histories admit. Henry Winstanley's Eddystone light, the first ever built on an open rock, was finished in 1698 and lasted five years. Winstanley had boasted that he would like to be inside it during the greatest storm that ever blew. He was, in November 1703, and neither he nor the tower was seen again.

Later builders learned from that. John Smeaton's Eddystone of 1759 was shaped like the trunk of an oak, wide at the root and curving inward, and its granite blocks were dovetailed into one another so that the whole tower behaved like a single stone. It stood for a hundred and twenty years and was taken down only because the reef beneath it was crumbling. The Stevensons in Scotland went further, designing towers whose curves were calculated so that a wave striking the base would be thrown outward rather than upward. Keepers still described green water breaking over the lantern, a hundred and thirty feet up.

Inside, a storm was mostly noise: a bass note in the stone, the whistle of the vent, the rifle-crack of spray on the panes. Keepers reported the tower trembling like a struck bell and the oil in the lamp shivering in its reservoir. The rule was that the light must not go out, and in practice this meant a keeper stood beside the lamp all night, wedged against the sway, relighting it if the draught snuffed it, cleaning the panes from the inside when he could no longer see the sea through them.

Some keepers went out into the weather, and those are the ones whose names survive. In September 1838 Grace Darling rowed with her father from the Longstone light, in the Farne Islands, to pull nine survivors from the wrecked Forfarshire. On the other side of the Atlantic, Ida Lewis of Lime Rock in Rhode Island was credited with eighteen rescues in a rowing boat over fifty years and was made keeper in her own right. Their fame was real, but it was also a little unfair to the thousands whose bravery took the form of simply staying at the lamp.

Towers and their storms
  • Eddystone, 1698. Winstanley's timber-and-stone tower; destroyed with its builder in the Great Storm of 1703.
  • Eddystone, 1759. Smeaton's dovetailed granite oak. Re-erected on Plymouth Hoe in 1884.
  • Bell Rock, 1811. Stevenson's tower on a tidal reef; the oldest surviving sea-washed lighthouse.
  • Skerryvore, 1844. Alan Stevenson; 156 feet of hyperbolic granite, twelve miles off Tiree.
IV

The Glass Beehive

The thing the keeper tended was, for most of the nineteenth century, one of the most sophisticated optical instruments on earth, and it lived at the top of a stone tube in the middle of the sea. A candle is visible for a mile or two. An oil lamp with a parabolic mirror behind it, the standard by 1800, threw perhaps a tenth of its light where it was needed. The rest warmed the ceiling.

In 1822 a French engineer named Augustin-Jean Fresnel proposed something different: surround the flame with rings of glass prisms, each one angled to catch light that would otherwise escape upward or downward and bend it into a flat horizontal sheet. The first of his lenses was lit at the Cordouan tower at the mouth of the Gironde in the summer of 1823. Sailors reported seeing it from a distance nobody had thought possible. Within thirty years every maritime nation had ordered them.

A first-order lens is the size of a small room, a beehive of brass and glass more than two and a half metres tall. Set on rollers or, later, floating on a bath of mercury, it turned around the lamp so that its bull's-eye panels swept the horizon like the spokes of a wheel, and each panel, passing, gave the ship a flash. The interval between flashes was the lighthouse's signature. A keeper at sea could name a light from its rhythm alone, the way you can name a friend by their footsteps on the stair.

The lens made the keeper a technician. He had to understand the flame's height to the millimetre, because the lamp's focus was fixed; a flame too tall or too short put the light into the sky or into the sea. He learned, without the vocabulary, the physics of refraction, and he learned it with his hands, at night, in a room that smelled of paraffin and Brasso.

Sidebar

The Fresnel Lens

Fresnel's insight was to replace one heavy curved lens with many thin rings, each ground to the angle that a full lens would have had at that radius. The result weighs a fraction as much, wastes almost no light, and can be built up like masonry from dozens of hand-polished elements.

Lenses were made in seven sizes, or orders, defined by the distance from the flame to the glass. First-order lenses lit the great landfall stations; sixth-order lenses marked harbour piers.

V

The Last Light Out

The end came slowly and then, as endings do, all at once. Electricity reached the towers between the wars, and an electric lamp does not need trimming. Timers replaced the keeper's match; photocells replaced the timers. By the 1960s the lens could be spun by a motor smaller than a sewing machine's, and a lamp-changer could swap a burnt-out bulb for a fresh one in the time it takes to blink. The tower no longer needed a witness. It needed a technician twice a year.

Automation did not switch the lights off. It switched the people off. Trinity House automated its last staffed lighthouse, North Foreland in Kent, in November 1998, and the Northern Lighthouse Board followed at Fair Isle South the same year. In the United States, where the Coast Guard had been withdrawing keepers since the 1960s, Boston Light in Massachusetts kept a resident keeper only because Congress wrote it into law. Almost everywhere else the keepers' cottages were sold as holiday lets, and the towers went on flashing to an audience that, thanks to satellite positioning, increasingly did not need to look up.

And yet the lights are still lit. Every maritime authority that has considered switching them off has decided not to, because the receiver on the bridge can fail, and the sky can be jammed, and a sailor in trouble at night still wants something in the world that does not depend on anything but glass and a bulb and a rule that was made two hundred years ago: that it must not go out.

The last keepers, interviewed in retirement, rarely spoke of heroism. They spoke about the brass and the stair and the sound the clockwork made. One said that what he missed was not the sea but the moment, half an hour before sunset, when he stood in a room made of glass with a match in his hand and the whole coast waiting.

The last keepers
  • 1823. First Fresnel lens lit at Cordouan, France.
  • 1900s. Incandescent oil-vapour burners triple the brightness of a first-order light.
  • 1960s. Automation programmes begin in the US, UK and Ireland.
  • 1998. North Foreland becomes the last Trinity House light to lose its keepers.
  • Today. Boston Light, lit since 1716, keeps a resident keeper by act of Congress.
