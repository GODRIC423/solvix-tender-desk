    A Wall in Twelve Stations · 1868 – 1985

    The Typewriter 1868–1985

    In 1868 three Milwaukee tinkerers patented a machine that promised to write faster than a pen. For the next hundred and seventeen years the typewriter set the rhythm of the office, the newsroom and the novel, until its own descendants, the word processor and the personal computer, put it on the shelf.

    This wall follows twelve turning points, one per station. Walk left to right. The carriage returns between chapters.

    Station 01
    I1868

    Milwaukee, Wisconsin · 23 June

    A patent for the Type-Writer

    Christopher Latham Sholes, Carlos Glidden and Samuel W. Soule receive U.S. Patent 79,265 for a “Type-Writer” built in a Milwaukee machine shop. The prototype has piano-style keys and prints one letter at a time onto paper held beneath a roller. Investor James Densmore buys a share and pushes Sholes through some thirty revisions before the design is fit to sell.

    Station 02
    II1874

    Ilion, New York · $125, capitals only

    Remington puts it on sale

    E. Remington & Sons, gunsmiths and sewing-machine makers, buy the rights in 1873 and ship the Sholes & Glidden Type-Writer the following year. It costs $125, prints capital letters only, and is a “blind writer”: the typist cannot see the line until the carriage is lifted. Its four-row keyboard carries the arrangement the world still calls QWERTY.

    Station 03
    III1878

    Remington No. 2 · upper and lower case

    The shift key

    The Remington No. 2 gives each typebar two characters, upper and lower case, selected by a new key that physically shifts the carriage. Typed text finally looks like print rather than a telegram. Through the 1880s business correspondence moves from copperplate hand to machine, and the typist becomes a profession, one filled overwhelmingly by women.

    Station 04
    IV1888

    Cincinnati, Ohio · 25 July

    The Cincinnati contest

    Frank McGurrin, a Salt Lake City stenographer who has memorised the QWERTY keyboard, races Louis Traub, who watches his fingers on a rival Caligraph, in a public typing contest. McGurrin wins decisively. Newspapers carry the result across the country, and touch typing, ten fingers with eyes on the copy, becomes the professional standard and the Remington layout its home.

    Station 05
    V1895

    Underwood No. 1 · front-strike

    Visible writing

    Franz Xaver Wagner’s front-strike mechanism lets the typebars swing up and hit the front of the platen, so the typist sees each line as it forms. John T. Underwood buys the design and puts the Underwood No. 1 into production in the mid-1890s. Refined into the No. 5, it becomes the template for nearly every manual typewriter of the twentieth century.

    Station 06
    VI1902

    Stamford, Connecticut · a motor, too early

    The first electric

    George Blickensderfer shows an electric typewriter with a motor-driven typewheel, decades ahead of its market. Few offices are wired for it, current is not standardised from town to town, and the machine is a commercial failure. The idea waits three decades for the grid to catch up.

    Station 07
    VII1935

    IBM · Rochester, New York

    IBM Electric Model 01

    Building on James Fields Smathers’s 1914 power-operated design and the Electromatic company it bought in 1933, IBM launches its first electric typewriter. A motor does the striking, so every letter lands with equal weight and a light touch types all day without fatigue. The electric office begins, and IBM has found a business.

    Station 08
    VIII1936

    Seattle · U.S. Patent 2,040,248

    The Dvorak layout

    August Dvorak and William Dealey are granted a patent for the Dvorak Simplified Keyboard, arranged so the most common letters sit on the home row under the strongest fingers. It is measurably efficient, and QWERTY holds anyway: the old layout is by now tens of millions of trained hands deep, and no office will retrain its typing pool for a better idea.

    Station 09
    IX1961

    IBM Selectric · 31 July

    The golf ball

    IBM replaces the basket of typebars with a single element the size of a golf ball that tilts and rotates to strike the paper. The carriage stays still and the ball travels instead, so the machine is faster, quieter and jam-proof. Housed in a body by Eliot Noyes, the Selectric ships with interchangeable typeballs, and one desk can change typeface in seconds.

    Station 10
    X1964

    IBM MT/ST · magnetic tape

    Words on tape

    The IBM Magnetic Tape Selectric Typewriter records every keystroke on a tape cartridge, so a letter can be corrected, stored and replayed at 150 words a minute without being retyped. IBM’s marketing department needs a name for what the machine does and settles on “word processing.” The typewriter has begun to remember.

    Station 11
    XI1978

    Olivetti · Ivrea, Italy

    Electronic

    Olivetti’s ET 101, designed by Mario Bellini, is the first electronic typewriter: a microprocessor, a daisy-wheel printhead, a one-line correction memory and a low sculpted housing where a basket of typebars used to be. It is nearly silent. Within a few years a small display appears above the keys, and the machine is quietly turning into a computer.

    Station 12
    XII1985

    Smith Corona · Brother · IBM Wheelwriter

    The last carriage

    Smith Corona and Brother sell typewriters with screens and disk drives and call them personal word processors. IBM’s Wheelwriter, launched the year before, is the company’s final typewriter family, and in 1991 the whole business is spun off to Lexmark. The machine that defined the office becomes a memory, a sound effect, and a keyboard layout that never left.
