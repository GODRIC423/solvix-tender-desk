§ 02

Reading the trace

01 · P-wave

The first arrival. Primary waves compress and stretch the rock along their path and travel fastest, so the pen twitches a little before anything dramatic happens. On this sheet they show up as a short, tight burst — a warning stitched into the baseline a fraction of a second before the sheet erupts.

02 · S-wave

Shear waves move the ground sideways and arrive second. This is the part everyone recognises: the needle is flung off the baseline in long, slow strokes. Their amplitude sets the magnitude reading in the log, and when a large event pins the arm against its stop the trace flattens — the recorder has clipped, not the earth.

03 · Coda

The long tail. Energy scattered off faults, basins and buildings keeps the ground ringing well after the main shocks pass, decaying roughly exponentially. The log's duration column measures from P onset until the trace sinks back into the noise floor — for a strong event that can be most of a screen of paper.

§ 03

Magnitude & the pen

MagnitudeClassWhat the pen does
M 2.0 – 2.9MicroA ripple barely above the building's hum. Logged, rarely felt.
M 3.0 – 3.9MinorClear spike a few millimetres tall, gone within a few seconds.
M 4.0 – 4.9LightDistinct P and S arrivals, a visible coda, a wobble in the arm.
M 5.0 – 5.9ModerateStrokes reach a third of the sheet; the log row is highlighted.
M 6.0 – 6.9StrongPen sweeps nearly edge to edge and starts to clip against the stops.
M 7.0 – 7.5MajorSaturated trace; flat tops, ink pooling, the whole drum shudders.

Magnitude is logarithmic. Each whole step on the scale is about a tenfold jump in the amplitude of ground motion and roughly thirty-two times the energy, which is why the pen behaves so differently between a 3 and a 6 — and why the hold gauge on this sheet rewards patience disproportionately.

Hold the paper and a ring fills around your pointer while the estimated magnitude climbs from 2.0 to 7.5 over about three seconds. Let go and the recorder injects a synthetic event: a short high-frequency P burst, an S-wave envelope shaped by the magnitude, then a coda that decays until it disappears back into the noise. Events shorter than a tap still register; they just stay small.

Background events arrive on their own every ten to twenty seconds, skewed towards the small end as real catalogues are. Nothing here is measured from a real sensor — the station is an instrument-shaped toy — but the shapes, timings and proportions are borrowed from how a drum recorder actually behaves.

Note for operators: the drum pauses when the tab is hidden and resumes cleanly. Under reduced-motion settings the paper advances at half speed and the arm no longer trembles at rest.
