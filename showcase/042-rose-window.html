I · Geometria

The geometry of twelve

A rose window is drawn before it is built, and it is drawn with two tools only: a compass and a straightedge. The master mason strikes a circle, keeps the same radius, and walks the compass point around the circumference. It lands exactly six times. Those six points make a hexagon; join every second one and two triangles appear, one upright and one inverted, the seal that mediaeval builders called the double triangle.

Bisect each of the six arcs and there are twelve. Every petal of the rose therefore sits in a sector of precisely thirty degrees, and the whole window becomes a clock without hands. Twelve is not a decorative choice. It is the number that the circle gives up freely to a compass, and it happens to be the count of months, apostles, tribes and hours that the window is meant to hold.

From there the design nests inward. The outer ring is halved again into twenty-four; the inner ring keeps the twelve; the centre resolves back to the original six. The oculus in the middle is the first circle the compass ever drew, still visible in the finished stone.

II · Color

The symbolism of colour

Stained glass is not stained. The colour is in the body of the glass itself, mixed into the molten pot with metal oxides — hence pot-metal glass. Each metal gives one hue, and each hue was read by the people beneath as a word. Four of those words are enough to write the entire window.

Ruby

Gold or copper in the melt · flashed thin

The Passion, martyrdom and charity. True ruby glass was so dense it was blown as a skin over clear glass, or it would have passed no light at all.

Sapphire

Cobalt · the blue of Chartres

Heaven, the Virgin, and faith in what is not seen. The deep cobalt of the twelfth century was never quite matched again once its recipe was lost.

Emerald

Copper and iron · the colour of spring

Hope, rebirth and the green earth of the third day. Green was the easiest hue to make and the hardest to make well; too much iron drags it toward bottle brown.

Amber

Silver stain fired on · from about 1300

Divine light, glory and the sun at meridian. Amber is the one colour the glazier could paint on afterwards, which is why haloes and hair suddenly turned gold in the fourteenth century.

III · Lux

Light passing through

Every other art is lit from the front. A fresco, a tapestry, a carved tympanum: light falls on them and bounces back to the eye. Glass alone is lit from behind. The image does not reflect the day, it is the day, filtered and sorted into colour. This is why a rose window looks black from the street and blazes from the nave, and why no photograph has ever quite carried it.

The glazier cut each piece with a hot iron and a grozing tool, painted the lines of faces and drapery in a brown iron pigment, fired them, and then leaded the pieces together with H-shaped strips of lead called cames, soldered at the joints and stiffened with iron saddle bars. The black lines are not a drawing style. They are the structure, and the structure is what lets the colour hang in the air.

Move across the window above and you will see the sun cross it: a band of light travels over the panes and each one wakes as it passes. That is the whole idea of the building, made small. Abbot Suger of Saint-Denis wrote that the glass should carry the mind from the material to the immaterial. He meant precisely this — colour that is nothing but light, held in place by lead.
