A note on vision

After Bridget Riley,
1931—

Gouache, graph paper,
measured intervals.

Rendered here in
two layers of
repeating gradient.

The eye is not a camera. It is an argument.

Put two rulings of black and white almost — but not exactly — on top of one another and something appears that neither of them contains. Broad pale bands wander across the plate, thickening, sliding, folding back. They have no ink. They are a beat frequency: the arithmetic of two intervals that refuse to agree, resolved by a retina that insists on finding edges everywhere.

The receptors in your eye do not report brightness. They report difference. A cell that sees light suppresses its neighbours — lateral inhibition — so that every boundary is exaggerated on arrival and every flat grey is quietly discarded. Feed that system a hundred boundaries per inch and it overshoots. The overshoot is the shimmer. Add the microsaccades your eyes make three times a second, tiny involuntary tremors that keep the image from fading, and the whole plate begins to swim, because each tremor lands the ruling on a slightly different bank of cells.

“I want the eye to be caught in the act of looking, before the mind has had time to name what it saw.”

Riley built her paintings from measurement, not from feeling: intervals plotted on graph paper, mixed by assistants, executed flat and matte so no brushstroke could distract from the interval itself. The result is not a trick, because a trick has a solution. Here the sensation is the subject. Cataract 3 does not depict water; it does to your eye roughly what water does — an unstable, glassy pull that you cannot switch off by knowing how it works.

The three plates below are the same argument in three grammars. Drive each one with the pointer, or with the keyboard on its slider, and watch how little movement it takes to change everything: a degree and a half of rotation, four pixels of interval, a fraction of a percent of scale.

Plate I

Beat

Two vertical rulings · difference

Two identical rulings, one rotated against the other and subtracted from it. The pale wedges that sweep the plate are not drawn — they are where the two intervals happen to fall out of step. At zero degrees the field goes flat and dead; at a degree and a half it is at its most unbearable. The breathing you can see even without touching it is the interval itself, widening and narrowing over seventeen seconds.

Plate II

Cataract

One ruling · displaced

A single horizontal ruling pushed through a continuous displacement field, so that each band bends without ever crossing its neighbour. This is the grammar of the water paintings: the wave never breaks, the crests only lean. Raise the amplitude and the ruling turns to fabric; the vertical position of the pointer sets how deep the pull runs, the horizontal position sets how long each swell is.

Plate III

Lattice

Two dot grids · rosette

Two square grids of dots, differenced. Where the grids coincide the plate is black; where they disagree it flares white, and the flares organise themselves into rosettes that rotate far faster than the grid that produced them. This is the printer's nightmare and the op painter's gift: a structure with a period many times larger than anything actually printed. Push the rotation past six degrees and the rosettes shrink into a fine, restless snow.

Method

Each plate is two layers of CSS repeating gradient, composited with mix-blend-mode: difference. The interval breathes on an animated background-size; the pointer writes its position into a custom property that rotates and scales the upper layer. Plate II adds one SVG turbulence displacement. No images, no libraries, no colour.

Warning

Sustained viewing of high-contrast rulings can provoke afterimages, eye strain and, rarely, discomfort in people sensitive to pattern. The vibration switch at the corner of the screen stops all autonomous motion; the page also honours your system's reduced-motion setting.
