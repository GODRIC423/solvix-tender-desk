Ten genera, read from the top of the troposphere down. Every cloud here is drawn from nothing but soft circles of light, and every one of them is telling you what the next day will bring, if you know how to listen.

01High étage

CirrusCi

cirrus — a lock of hair

  • Altitude6 – 12 km
  • Made ofIce crystals
  • BringsFair, for now

Detached white filaments, silky and fibrous, often hooked at one end where the ice falls into slower wind. On its own, cirrus means fine weather. Watch its direction: if the streaks thicken from the west and spread into a sheet over the afternoon, a warm front is roughly a day away.

Hooked tips point along the wind at altitude, the fastest air you can see.

The old sailors called them mares' tails and reefed sail if they multiplied.

Specimen · High cloud · No precipitation reaches ground
Hooked head Fibrous streak

02High étage

Cirro­cumulusCc

the mackerel sky

  • Altitude6 – 12 km
  • Made ofIce & supercooled water
  • BringsChange, soon

A thin white sheet broken into tiny grains and ripples, none of them wider than a finger held at arm's length, none of them shaded. It is the rarest of the high clouds and the least patient: cirrocumulus is a cloud in transition, usually on its way to becoming cirrus or cirrostratus within the hour.

"Mackerel sky, mackerel sky — never long wet, never long dry."

The ripples are gravity waves made visible; the air aloft is unstable.

Specimen · High cloud · Elements under 1° across
Unshaded grains Ripple rows

03High étage

Cirro­stratusCs

the veil with a halo

  • Altitude6 – 12 km
  • Made ofIce crystals
  • BringsRain in 12–24 h

A milky veil so thin you may not notice it at all, until you see the sun wearing a perfect ring. That 22° halo is light bent through hexagonal ice, and it is the most reliable forecast in this guide: a warm front is coming. The veil will thicken into altostratus, then the rain begins.

Shadows on the ground stay sharp; the light is only slightly dimmed.

"A ring around the sun or moon means rain or snow is coming soon."

Specimen · High cloud · Halo-forming
22° halo Milky veil

04Middle étage

Alto­cumulusAc

the sheep of the sky

  • Altitude2 – 7 km
  • Made ofWater droplets
  • BringsSettled, unless it towers

Rolls and patches of white and grey, each element one to three fingers wide and, unlike cirrocumulus, clearly shaded on its underside. Most days it means nothing but a pleasant, dappled light. But on a humid summer morning, altocumulus with little turrets on top is a warning: the air is ready to convect, and thunderstorms are likely by afternoon.

Turreted variety is called castellanus; look for it before ten in the morning.

Lens-shaped Ac lenticularis hangs downwind of mountains, motionless.

Specimen · Middle cloud · Elements 1–5° across
Sunlit crown Shaded underside

05Middle étage

Alto­stratusAs

sun through ground glass

  • Altitude2 – 7 km
  • Made ofWater & ice
  • BringsRain within hours

A featureless grey-blue sheet across the whole sky. The sun shows through it as a pale disc with no edge, as if seen through frosted glass, and nothing on the ground casts a shadow. This is the warm front arriving: the cirrostratus of yesterday has thickened and lowered, and the first drops are usually only a few hours away.

No halo — the droplets are too big and too mixed with ice to bend the light cleanly.

If the sun disappears entirely, reclassify: you are under nimbostratus now.

Specimen · Middle cloud · Shadowless light
Sun, no edge Uniform sheet

06Middle étage, low base

Nimbo­stratusNs

nimbus — the rain-bearer

  • Altitude0.5 – 5 km
  • Made ofWater, ice, falling rain
  • BringsSteady rain, all day

Dark, thick, and completely without feature, a cloud that hides the sun and often the hilltops too. Nimbostratus is not a forecast; it is the weather itself. Its rain or snow is continuous and moderate, never a downpour, and it will persist for hours while the warm front passes overhead. Ragged scraps of low cloud drift beneath it.

Steady rain with no thunder and no gusts is the signature of a frontal system.

Brightening in the west, late in the day, means the front is nearly through.

Specimen · Precipitating · Base often below 2 km
Featureless base Continuous rain

07Low étage

Strato­cumulusSc

the lumpy grey blanket

  • Altitude0.5 – 2 km
  • Made ofWater droplets
  • BringsDull, mostly dry

Large rounded rolls and slabs of grey and white, packed together with thin gaps of sky between them, each lump wider than a fist held at arm's length. It is the most common cloud on Earth and one of the least dramatic: a dull day, but usually a dry one. It forms when a layer of moist air is capped and can only spread sideways.

Often the last act of a rainy day, as nimbostratus breaks up behind the front.

Light drizzle is possible; anything heavier means something else is above it.

Specimen · Low cloud · Elements over 5° across
Rounded roll Grey underside

08Low étage

StratusSt

fog that has lifted a little

  • Altitude0 – 0.5 km
  • Made ofWater droplets
  • BringsDrizzle, then clearing

A uniform grey layer with a base so low it swallows hilltops, towers and the upper floors of anything tall. Stratus is fog that has been nudged a few hundred metres off the ground by a light wind. It brings a damp, dim morning and perhaps a fine drizzle, but where it forms overnight under clear skies it usually burns off by late morning.

If the sun shows through, its outline is sharp — unlike altostratus.

Common on coasts, where sea air slides in cool over warm land.

Specimen · Low cloud · Hides the hilltops
Uniform base Hilltop lost

09Low étage

CumulusCu

cumulus — a heap

  • Altitude0.5 – 2 km base
  • Made ofWater droplets
  • BringsFair, if it stays small

The cloud a child draws: brilliant white cauliflower tops, a flat grey base, hard edges, blue sky all around. Each one is a rising column of warm air made visible, born mid-morning as the ground heats and gone by dusk. Small and wider than tall, they mean fair weather. Taller than wide by noon, and still growing, they mean showers by three.

All the bases sit at the same height: the level where rising air reaches its dew point.

Cumulus humilis is the fair-weather kind; congestus is the one to watch.

Specimen · Low cloud · Convective, daytime
Rising column Dew-point base

10All three étages

Cumulo­nimbusCb

the tower with an anvil

  • Altitude0.5 – 13 km
  • Made ofWater, ice, hail
  • BringsThunderstorm, now

A cumulus that would not stop. The tower climbs through every level of the troposphere until it hits the stratosphere and flattens into an anvil of ice, streaming downwind. Beneath it come heavy rain, hail, lightning and violent gusts as cold air collapses out of the base. Seek shelter; a single cell usually passes within the hour.

Count seconds between flash and thunder and divide by three for the kilometres.

The anvil points the direction the storm is travelling. Do not be under it.

Specimen · Vertical development · Hazardous
Anvil, 13 km Downdraught

Nº 81Colophon

The ten genera follow the World Meteorological Organization's International Cloud Atlas. Altitudes are for temperate latitudes; in the tropics the high étage begins nearer 6 km and ends nearer 18. Weather meanings are the old observers' rules, still worth knowing when the phone has no signal.

Every cloud on this page is built from layered circles of blurred colour and nothing else — no photographs, no images. Set in Figtree. Skies run from pale dawn to storm grey.
