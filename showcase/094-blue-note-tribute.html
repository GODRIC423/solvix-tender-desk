The label

Blue Note Records was founded in New York in 1939 by Alfred Lion, a German émigré who had heard boogie-woogie at a Carnegie Hall concert and decided the music deserved better treatment than it was getting. Between 1956 and 1967 the label's covers were designed almost entirely by one man, Reid Miles, who worked from Francis Wolff's session photographs and a small type case, and who by his own admission barely listened to jazz.

Miles cropped faces to the brow, ran headlines off the edge of the board, and set whole sleeves in two inks because the budget allowed for two inks. The records inside were cut by Rudy Van Gelder in his parents' living room in Hackensack, then in a purpose-built studio in Englewood Cliffs. This night borrows all of it: the horn, the blue, the crop, the crackle.

The sleeve

Set in Bebas Neue and Inter Tight. Three inks only, black, blue and paper, with no gradients anywhere, the way a two-colour offset job would have been priced in 1961. The catalogue number is BLP 4094 because this is the ninety-fourth sleeve in a series of one hundred, and the 4000 series is where the label kept its best stereo pressings.

The crackle you hear when your cursor crosses the lineup is synthesised in the browser: filtered white noise for the surface hiss, a click once per revolution for the scratch that every loved record has, and randomly timed pops for the dust. Nothing is sampled. Move away and the needle lifts.
