i · Preface

Why these, and why in winter

Every reading list is an argument disguised as a list. This one argues that the Gothic novel, which we are so often told is about castles, is in truth about attention: about what a mind does to itself when the room is dark, the hour is late, and the only light is a small one that moves. The winter term is the right season for it. The afternoons close at four; the reading room empties by six; the radiators knock like something trying to get in.

We begin with the book that invented the genre almost by accident, follow it through the century that took it seriously, and finish with two books that are not Gothic at all but are the oldest remedies we have for the moods the Gothic cultivates. Read in order. Bring a pencil. Write in the margins — the copies in the Old Library have been written in for two hundred years and are the better for it.

Editions are not prescribed. All ten texts are in the public domain and may be had free from Project Gutenberg or the Internet Archive, or from the second-hand tables outside the Covered Market for less than the price of a coffee.

Attention, not architecture. Say this to anyone who tells you the course is about castles.

The pencil rule is not a suggestion. Ink is for people who are sure.

ii · The Ten Books

The list, annotated

Book I · Week 1

The Castle of Otranto

Horace Walpole 1764

A helmet the size of a carriage falls out of the sky and kills a bridegroom on page two, and the book never once apologises. Walpole wrote it in a fortnight after a dream, printed it as a "translation" from a medieval Italian manuscript, and only admitted authorship when it sold. Everything the genre would later do with straight face — the labyrinthine passage, the portrait that sighs, the usurper undone by his own ancestor — is here, done at a gallop and half in jest.

Read it fast, as it was written. Note how often the word terror appears and how rarely anyone is actually hurt by it.

OriginShort

The second preface, where he drops the mask, is the real essay of the course. Read it twice.

Book II · Week 2

Northanger Abbey

Jane Austen 1817, written c. 1798

Catherine Morland has read too many novels of the Otranto kind and arrives at a perfectly comfortable country house expecting a corpse in every cabinet. Austen's joke is gentle but it is also structural: the horror Catherine eventually finds is not supernatural but social, in the form of a general who turns a young woman out of his house because she is poorer than he thought. The parody, in other words, turns out to be a Gothic novel after all.

Pair the laundry-list scene in the cabinet with Walpole's helmet. Which is the more frightening object, and why?

Parody

"The person, be it gentleman or lady, who has not pleasure in a good novel, must be intolerably stupid." Put it on the door.

Book III · Week 3

Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus

Mary Shelley 1818 text

Please read the 1818 edition and not the 1831 revision, which softens Victor and blames fate for what the earlier book blames on a man who would not look at what he had made. The novel is a set of nested letters — a sailor writing to his sister, a scientist confessing to the sailor, a creature pleading with the scientist — and its argument lives in that structure: every voice is being overheard, and no one is speaking to the person who most needs to listen.

The creature's education by eavesdropping on the De Lacey cottage is the most tender passage in the syllabus. Attend to what he reads.

Central1818 text

He learns to read from Paradise Lost, Plutarch and Werther. A worse reading list than this one, and it made him what he is.

Book IV · Week 4

Wuthering Heights

Emily Brontë 1847

The only book on this list that is set entirely in weather. Lockwood, a soft southern tenant, is trapped by snow at the Heights and dreams of a branch tapping at the glass that turns out to be a small cold hand. From there the novel hands itself to Nelly Dean, the housekeeper, who tells the whole history of two houses over several evenings by the fire — which is to say that the book is itself a winter reading, performed aloud to a man who cannot leave.

Keep a family tree as you go. Everyone is called Catherine, Linton or Heathcliff, and the confusion is on purpose.

Central

Nelly is not a neutral narrator. Count how many times she "happens" to be at the door.

Book V · Week 5

Carmilla

J. Sheridan Le Fanu 1872

A quarter of a century before Stoker, Le Fanu wrote the vampire story that Stoker would raid for furniture, and did it in a hundred pages. Laura, alone in a schloss in Styria, is befriended by a languid guest who sleeps until the afternoon and is never seen to pray. The novella is remarkable for its sympathy: Carmilla is a predator, but the book cannot quite bring itself to hate her, and neither can Laura, and neither, if we are honest, can we.

A short week. Use the spare time to begin Burton, who is long.

Novella

The doctor's "hysteria" diagnosis is the first of several in the term where a man names a thing so as not to look at it.

Book VI · Week 6

The Anatomy of Melancholy

Robert Burton 1621; selections

Not a novel, not Gothic, and not to be read from cover to cover by anyone with a life. Burton, an Oxford divine who never left his college, wrote a medical treatise on sadness that swelled through six editions into the most digressive book in English. We read "Democritus Junior to the Reader", the causes of melancholy from Part I, and the "Cure by Study" from Part II. He is our first physician: he believes that the disease we have spent five weeks enjoying can be treated by reading — and by the right reading, which he lists.

Falls in the reading week. Take your time; he did.

CureSelections

"I write of melancholy, by being busy to avoid melancholy." The whole method in a sentence.

Book VII · Week 7

The Picture of Dorian Gray

Oscar Wilde 1890 magazine text

Back to the disease, now in evening dress. Wilde's only novel takes the Gothic device of the portrait that changes and turns it into a book about looking — at art, at other people, at oneself in the glass. Read the shorter 1890 Lippincott's text rather than the padded 1891 book: it is franker, faster and has not yet had its epigrams tidied into a preface. The poisonous "yellow book" that ruins Dorian is never named, and the seminar will spend some time on why.

Central1890 text

Basil paints; Henry talks; Dorian is looked at. Three ways of being a reader.

Book VIII · Week 8

The Turn of the Screw

Henry James 1898

A governess arrives at a country house with two beautiful children and begins to see, on the tower and across the lake, two figures who should be dead. Whether the ghosts are real or the governess is unwell is the most argued question in the genre and James, who was asked repeatedly, declined ever to say. The book is written so that both readings are fully supported by the text, and the seminar will be conducted on the principle that anyone who is certain has not read carefully.

Note the frame: another fireside, another manuscript, another audience that cannot leave.

Central

Compare Lockwood at the Heights, Walton on the ice, Laura in Styria. Every narrator is snowed in somewhere.

Book IX · Week 9

Middlemarch, Books I–IV

George Eliot 1871–72

The great anti-Gothic novel, and our second cure. Dorothea Brooke marries a scholar she imagines to be a locked cabinet of learning and finds, in the Casaubon chapters, that the horror of a bad marriage is not a secret but the absence of one. Eliot takes every Gothic instrument — the moulder-ing manuscript, the tyrannical old man, the young wife in the cold house — and shows that they are ordinary, which is worse. We read the first four books this term and finish in the spring.

CurePart one

Casaubon's Key to All Mythologies is Burton's Anatomy gone wrong: a book that eats its author instead of curing him.

Book X · Week 10

Meditations

Marcus Aurelius c. 170–180; trans. George Long, 1862

Written in Greek by a Roman emperor on campaign, in a tent, at night, for no reader but himself. The Meditations are the plainest book on the list and the last for a reason: after ten weeks of minds working on themselves in the dark, here is a mind working on itself in the dark to a different end. Long's Victorian translation is stiff but free, and its stiffness suits the material. Read Books II, IV and XII, and whatever else the candle allows.

Final seminar. Bring the pencil-marked copies of everything. We will compare margins.

CureLast

"Look within. Within is the fountain of good, and it will ever bubble up, if thou wilt ever dig." — VII.59. A reading list for one.

iv · A Note on Candlelight

On reading by a small light

Nine of these ten books were written for readers who had no other light. A single tallow candle gives about one candela, which is roughly one eightieth of the illumination on an ordinary desk today. Within that circle — a foot or two across — the page is bright and warm; outside it the room does not exist. This is not a detail of period atmosphere. It is the reading condition the books assume, and it shapes them: the short chapter, the cliffhanger at the end of a sitting, the narrator who breaks off because "the candle is guttering and I must close."

Whilst I read, the wind blew, and the taper went out, and I was left with the page half-finished in my hand and the whole house awake around me.

— compiler's notebook, from a reading of Wuthering Heights, some winter ago

The light on this page is an attempt to give the condition back. It follows your hand as a candle would follow the reader who carries it; it flickers because tallow flickers; what is far from it stays in shadow. You will find that you read differently under it — more slowly, one paragraph at a time, with the rest of the list held in the dark until you go to it. That is how these books were meant to be met. If you would rather have the room lit, the button in the running head will snuff it, and no one will think the less of you.

Practical notes for the term. Read in the evening if you can, and by one lamp rather than the ceiling light. Read a chapter at a sitting, not a chapter and a half. Stop when the book stops. And write in the margin before you turn the page, because the thought that waits until morning is a different thought.

A "candle" is an old unit for a reason. The whole science of light began by asking how far from the flame one could still read.

Gutter, v.: of a candle, to melt away rapidly through a channel in the wax. The word came to writing from wax, not the other way.

Snuffed, not blown. A blown candle smokes.
