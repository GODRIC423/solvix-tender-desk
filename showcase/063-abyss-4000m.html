Abyss

to 4,000 m

Below you is the largest habitat on Earth, and almost none of it has ever been lit. Scroll to descend. The meter is your depth; the light is yours to aim. Most of what lives down here will show itself only where the beam finds it.

I

Zone one · epipelagic

Sunlight

0 – 200 m

Everything the ocean grows, it grows here. Sunlight fades exponentially with depth: at 200 metres roughly one percent of it remains, too little for photosynthesis. Even so, this thin lit skin holds the plankton that feeds every animal below it, and bioluminescence already begins at the surface, on nights when the waves sparkle.

0 – 50 mBioluminescent

Sea sparkle

Noctiluca scintillans · dinoflagellate

A single-celled organism about a millimetre across that flashes blue when the water around it is disturbed. In a dense bloom a footstep in wet sand, a paddle stroke or a breaking wave sets off millions at once. The flash is a startle response: a sudden light that scares off the tiny grazers trying to eat it, or draws in something bigger to eat the grazer.

0 – 200 mBioluminescent

Warty comb jelly

Mnemiopsis leidyi · ctenophore

By day the eight rows of beating cilia scatter sunlight into shifting rainbows. By night the same animal produces its own blue-green glow when it is touched. It has no stinging cells, no brain and no bones, yet when ballast water carried it into the Black Sea in the 1980s it ate enough fish eggs and larvae to collapse an anchovy fishery.

II

Zone two · mesopelagic

Twilight

200 – 1,000 m

Light still reaches this far, but only as a faint blue glow from above, and nothing can grow on it. Instead the twilight zone is the ocean's great commuter belt: each night the largest animal migration on the planet rises hundreds of metres to feed near the surface, and sinks again before dawn. Camouflage here means matching the glow overhead so you cast no silhouette.

300 – 1,200 mBioluminescent

Lanternfish

Myctophidae · around 250 species

Probably the most abundant vertebrates on Earth: by some estimates lanternfish make up well over half of all deep-sea fish biomass. Rows of photophores along the belly emit a dim blue light tuned to the downwelling glow, erasing the fish's shadow for anything hunting from below. Their nightly migration is dense enough that early sonar operators mistook it for a false sea floor.

200 – 1,000 mBioluminescent

Hatchetfish

Argyropelecus · deep-sea hatchetfishes

A body no thicker than a coin, plated with mirror-like scales that reflect the surrounding blue so the fish nearly disappears from the side. Tubular eyes point straight up, scanning for the silhouettes of prey against the last of the light. Underneath, a keel of photophores runs counter-illumination, dimming and brightening as clouds pass far above.

III

Zone three · bathypelagic

Midnight

1,000 – 4,000 m

No sunlight at all. From here down the only light anywhere is made by living things, and around three quarters of the animals here can make it. The water settles near four degrees; pressure passes one hundred atmospheres. Food arrives as marine snow, a slow blizzard of dead plankton, mucus and faecal pellets drifting down from the productive water far above.

1,000 – 2,000 mBioluminescent

Humpback anglerfish

Melanocetus johnsonii · female

The lure on her forehead is a modified fin ray whose tip is packed with luminous symbiotic bacteria. She waves it, waits, and swallows whatever comes to investigate; a stomach that stretches lets her take prey longer than herself. Only females grow this large, roughly a hand's length. Males are a few centimetres, have no lure at all, and spend their lives searching the dark for a mate.

500 – 5,000 mBioluminescent

Atolla jellyfish

Atolla wyvillei · crown jellyfish

Deep red by day, which reads as black down here, so it is nearly invisible until something attacks it. Then it lights up in a rotating pinwheel of blue flashes: a burglar alarm meant to attract a larger predator to whatever is biting it. An electronic lure imitating this display was what drew the first giant squid ever filmed in its natural habitat, in 2012.

IV

Zone four · abyssal plain

Abyssal

4,000 m

The abyssal plain. Pressure is roughly four hundred times what you feel at the surface; the water holds a steady one to two degrees and has been out of contact with the sky for centuries. Yet this is not empty. The sediment is worked over by animals that have never encountered daylight and would not know what to do with it. Your light is the first many of them have ever seen.

3,000 – 4,000 mNot luminous

Dumbo octopus

Grimpoteuthis · deepest-living octopus

Named for the pair of ear-like fins it flaps to hover just above the sea floor, this is the deepest-living octopus genus known: one was filmed at almost 7,000 metres in the Java Trench in 2020. It has no ink sac, having nothing to hide from in the dark, and swallows worms and crustaceans whole. Most are about the size of a shoe. Without your light there is nothing here to see at all.

1,000 – 4,700 mNot luminous

Tripod fish

Bathypterois grallator · standing fish

It stands. Three fin rays stiffen into stilts up to a metre long, lifting a thirty-centimetre body above the mud, facing into the current with its pectoral fins fanned out like antennae to feel for drifting food. Its eyes are almost useless here; touch does the work. When it needs to move, the rays go slack and it swims.

You are at 4,000 metres. Above you is four kilometres of water; below, sediment that has been settling since before there were people. The ocean's average depth is about 3,700 m, so most of the planet's floor looks like this: dark, cold, slow, and quietly alive.
